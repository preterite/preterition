A note found on a workbench, dated May 2026, proposes a trait with no curriculum: the capacity to take in less on purpose. Its author trained in the 1990s under AirLand Battle doctrine, when to see more was to win. Now, watching drones over Ukraine, he wonders whether the advantage has flipped, whether a soldier survives by managing his own signature, giving off less, and by ignoring most of what the sensors bring him. The note calls this disciplined reduction. Then, almost in passing, it admits that the trait "cannot be taught analytically."

I begin with that admission, because a list of points will not teach it either.

Consider a different scene. A man sits by the Ilissus with a scroll under his cloak, and the story he tells warns that writing will give us the appearance of wisdom while our memory weakens. The weblog post that sends me back to the *Phaedrus* calls this "old, well-trod ground," and then recommends one more reading anyway. Rehearsal is useful, it says.

So this is a rehearsal. Here is the problem as I see it so far: every technology that extends what we can take in also asks us to decide what to let go. The scroll, the slip box, the sensor feed. What I am asking is whether the letting go can be practiced, and by whom, and at whose cost.

Start with *at whose cost*, since block 1 left it hanging.

A weblog post from 2010, written by someone with an RSS reader open and too much in it, objects to calling attention a commodity: "It's a form of work; of labor." If he is right, then taking in less on purpose is not just a quiet act of willpower. It is work, and someone does it, unpaid or otherwise. A feed that "feels like firehose force" doesn't get any smaller because one reader looks away. Somebody else has to do the sorting.

Sixteen years later the workbench note catches its own risk and names it plainly. Telling people to "be less visible," it says, "is not a neutral skill" when the people told are women, students of color, or contingent faculty. The soldier manages his signature to stay alive. The adjunct manages hers to stay employed. It's the same verb, but the stakes behind it are different.

So I hold two pictures side by side and keep both. In one, a man by the river chooses his scroll. In the other, a woman at a seminar table has learned not to raise her hand. Both of them are taking in less. Only one of them is choosing it.

Who does the sorting, then? Marx answered in 1858, a long time before the RSS reader. In the automatic factory the workers are "cast merely as its conscious linkages." What they do is narrowed down until it only passes along the machine's motion: it "supervises it and guards against interruptions." I read that sentence three times and kept seeing the reader of a feed, refreshing it. He isn't making anything. He is keeping the flow going.

The 2010 post nearly reaches this point and then turns off. It says digital tools replace "labor-intensive processes with capital-intensive processes," and it stops there. But the aggregator doesn't do away with the labor. It moves it onto the conscious linkage, the person at the end of the hose.

A note in the margin, to myself: the same post brushes aside its own best example. Someone keeps a child comfortable, turns the pages, and holds the book at eye level. "That's not multitasking," it says. "That's reading a bedtime story." Nobody counts this kind of attention as work because the person doing it is fond of the one receiving it.

So here is a third picture to set beside the man by the river and the woman who keeps her hand down. Someone sits with a book open on her lap and keeps three kinds of attention going at once, and nobody calls any of it labor.

An attempted ledger, kept in the margin under that third picture:

Debit: one book held at eye level.\\
Debit: pages turned at the pace of a smaller reader.\\
Debit: one question about the dog, put off until the end of the page.\\
Credit: none entered. Nobody has a column for it.

The workbench note has its own version of this missing column. It says institutions reward "visible effort, comprehensive plans, detailed briefs," and so "reduction appears as hesitation." I think the two blindnesses are related. The parent who keeps the story going and the sergeant who decides what not to look at are both doing work that shows only when it fails. When it goes right, nothing appears to have happened.

Marx's conscious linkage has the same problem in a harsher form. It guards against interruptions, and the machine takes the credit for running smoothly.

Luhmann offers a way out, or seems to. He treats his slip box as a partner in conversation, not a hose, and he says the first condition of talking with it is that "the partners can mutually surprise each other." A feed can't surprise you, because it never stops arriving. A box you have to dig through sometimes can. So maybe the difference between attention as labor and attention as thought is whether anything ever answers back.

I don't know yet whether that rescues the parent. A child answers back all the time.

So, a second try at the ledger. This time the column is for answers, not costs.

Interruption: "Is that the same dog?" Filed as a debit. The page waits.\\
Interruption: "Why is he sad?" Debit. The page waits again.\\
Surprise: "That's not what it said last time." Where does this one go?

The 2015 post suggests a way to sort them. Read a strange text aloud with colleagues, it says, and mark "only the moments where things got weird," then ask whether those are "places that are problematic, or places that are interesting." It's the same pencil and the same mark, with two different ledgers. The writer even leaves a gap in his own article on purpose, so the reader has to say "Wait, what?" and go back.

I think that's the difference I was looking for. An interruption breaks the flow. A surprise breaks the reading itself, the way it breaks when the child catches you skipping a page you thought she wouldn't notice. Calvino's tribal storyteller works through "jaguars and toucans" one combination at a time until one harmless tale "explodes into a terrible revelation." He didn't plan it. The listeners, with the ghosts they bring with them, made it happen.

So the parent's work is partly the work of a filter, telling interruption from surprise while the page is still open. That sorting is invisible too. But some days it is the reason to read the book at all.

A third entry for the ledger, and I'm still not sure which column it belongs in.

Someone pasted a workbench note into my file. It's about soldiers, not parents, but it names the skill of the filter outright: "*knowing what not to look at.*" The note calls it disciplined reduction and treats it as the scarce virtue of an age with too much signal, "refusal to chase noise." I agree with it for about a paragraph. Then I picture it at the bedside. If I'm a good reducer, "Is that the same dog?" gets ignored and the page turns on time.

Luhmann pulls the other way. He writes about his card file as if it were a colleague, and he puts a condition on any partnership: "the partners can mutually surprise each other." Information exists only for someone who can compare one message with what else might have come, "this or something else." The child has that comparison ready. She remembers what it said last time.

So two disciplines pull against each other at the open book. One says to cut. The other says the cut is where the meaning lives. I don't think the reader on the floor gets to pick one of them once and for all. She has to pick again on every page, and nobody calls that work.

Back to the floor, then, and the question I left there: "Is that the same dog?"

A 2015 weblog post gives me a way to sort the interruption that doesn't need either column. Its author imagines a room of composition instructors reading Kinbote's foreword from *Pale Fire* out loud, with one instruction: mark "only the moments where things got weird for you as a reader." Then comes the question I needed: "Do you characterize those moments as places that are problematic, or places that are interesting?"

That's the choice the reader makes on every page, said more plainly. The disciplined reducer counts the dog question as noise, a problem to manage. The pencil counts it as a mark that the story just jumped tracks, and that a small reader caught it.

The same post admits its author wants to leave a confusing moment in his own article, "a place where it switches tracks," because it makes difficulty right where difficulty is the subject. I'm tempted to do the same here. This block doesn't settle block 6. It marks a weird spot in it.

Calvino says the more brightly we light our houses, "the more their walls ooze ghosts." A perfectly filtered page would be a very bright house. The child's question is the ghost. It comes back with whatever the filter decided not to look at.

So maybe the discipline isn't cutting or keeping. Maybe it's a pencil, a mark in the margin that says *come back here*.

A pencil mark only works if somebody comes back to it.

Niklas Luhmann built a whole working life around coming back. He kept a cabinet of index cards and called it "a competent partner of communication." He insisted the partnership had a test: "the partners can mutually surprise each other." He filed a card years earlier, forgot what it said, and when he found it again it told him something he didn't know. In the terms of block 7, the slip box is the margin mark grown into furniture. It is a filter that keeps the ghosts in drawers instead of throwing them out.

It's worth noticing that the workbench note does the same thing. Near its end, below its outline of five axes of contradiction, it has a heading: "Live questions for return." The man who wants to teach taking in less doesn't delete what he can't use yet. He labels it *later*. Elsewhere he writes that a piece arguing for disciplined reduction "has to itself practice disciplined reduction." His way of doing that turns out to be deferral, not deletion.

So I can now separate two kinds of reduction. One cuts the dog question. The other puts it on a card. The first makes the house brighter. The second gives the ghosts somewhere to live.

Of course, someone has to keep the cabinet.

Who keeps it? Luhmann answers without meaning to, and his answer reads like a list of chores:

Number every slip, permanently.\\
When you link one card to another, write the back link on the other card "right away."\\
Keep a register of keywords "that we constantly update," because "we cannot rely on our memory of numbers."\\
File what you actually read in a separate box from what you only meant to read.

Only after "extensive work with this technique," he says, does the alter ego appear. The partner that surprises you was built one card at a time, by the person it later surprises. Deferral is not free. A drawer of ghosts needs housekeeping.

Calvino says the same thing about his literature machine. It can run every permutation of jaguars and toucans, but the shock "occurs only if the writing machine is surrounded by the hidden ghosts" of a particular, historical person. The cabinet doesn't surprise everyone. It surprises the one who kept it.

So I go back to the bedside. "Is that the same dog?" The child is keeping a register. She has a back link the adult never wrote down. The adult's work there is to treat the child's memory as the cabinet and to look up the card when she points to it. This is the same work as maintaining the slip box, and it pays the same, which is nothing.

Block 10:\\
One more card for block 9's drawer, filed under *who keeps it*, cross-referenced to *at whose cost*.

In Mike Edwards's play about a hall where nothing may be written down, the working notes reach a cold conclusion: "the man with the money is the archive." The patron finishes everyone else's half-lines. The room had taken that for wit, and the notes call it "retention demonstrated in public," which no one has read as a threat. *Money is memory; the patron remembers.*

So the register is not innocent either. Whoever keeps it decides which card turns up when somebody points. At the bedside the child holds the back link and the adult looks it up. That is a fair arrangement, because the keeper there is the smaller one. In the hall it runs the other way. The one who remembers can quote you back to yourself, and the rest of the room has nothing written to check him against.

The 2003 post puts its finger on the instrumental fantasy: "we write with it and then we put the pencil down," and we come away unchanged. Luhmann would not believe that, and neither would Aeryn, who finally takes up the pen and writes down her own misreading.

The question I'm left with: is disciplined reduction safe only when you aren't the one who keeps the archive? If you let go, someone else is still remembering.

A provisional answer to block 10's question, written on the back of the same card.

Look at what Aeryn actually does. In the working notes she doesn't win the argument. She takes the pen and writes down her own misreading. By then there are four versions of what she said, and the notes point out that this one, the fourth, "is the only one that survives, in her hand, wrong." She can't correct it without giving something away. But it's hers. Methis can still quote her back to herself, only now there's something in writing to check him against.

That seems to me the condition the workbench note leaves unspoken. It lists one ideal trait, "delegation without abdication," and it means the squad leader who passes on a task without giving up the outcome. The same goes for memory. You can let most of the feed go and leave the archive with somebody else, as long as you keep one card of your own. It can be small. It doesn't even have to be right.

Ledger, revised:\\
Let go: the firehose, the comprehensive brief, the full transcript.\\
Kept: one line, in my own hand, about what I took the moment to mean.

So I'd add a clause to *disciplined reduction*: take in less, but write down what you misread. Whoever keeps no record of their own mistakes is stuck with the patron's version of them.

Whether a sergeant under drones has time to keep a card is the next question to put to the note.

So, the sergeant. Does he have time to keep a card?

The note doesn't say so outright, but its answer seems to be no. It wants squads that "act under degraded communications," with "reduced radio chatter." Its ideal Ranger is an "exposure-time accountant," and for him every word sent is a signature someone can pick up. Writing is emission too. In the play the hall forbids inscription, so the patron becomes the archive. Over the field it works the other way round: the drone overhead is the archive. It records every pause and every cluster of bodies. The man underneath keeps nothing, and the enemy has the full transcript.

So I imagine the card he could keep. It wouldn't be written during the movement. It would come after, when the squad has gone to ground:

FIELD CARD, NOT FOR TRANSMISSION \\
What I ignored: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ \\
What I took it to mean: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ \\
Was I wrong: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

It's three lines with no encryption, and nobody grades it. It's Aeryn's fourth version, written in the dirt, wrong. Nobody is going to fill in that third blank honestly unless someone above him has decided that a misreading put on record counts as work.

Maybe this is how the trait gets taught after all, if it "cannot be taught analytically." It wouldn't come from a lecture on what to ignore. It would come from a habit of writing down, afterward, what you ignored and how you got it wrong, and from an institution willing to read those cards instead of the comprehensive brief.

But who reads the field card, and with what kind of pencil?

If the card goes up the chain to be graded, the third blank fills itself in: *No.* A misreading on file is a liability, so the soldier learns to give off less on paper too. That is signature discipline again, this time aimed at his own side. The workbench note lists the behaviors the grading culture "may need to reward," and honest error is not among them. Its Ranger is an accountant, and accountants know which entries get audited.

The 2015 post imagines a different reader. Composition instructors sit around a table with Kinbote's foreword from *Pale Fire* and mark "only the moments where things got weird." They don't decide beforehand whether a mark means a fault or a find. Try the same thing with the sergeant: after the patrol, the cards are read aloud in a circle, and *Was I wrong: yes* starts a conversation without ending a career. The post asks whether we might be "more fascinated by students." The sergeant would have to be fascinated in the same way by his soldiers' mistakes.

The note has a phrase that almost covers this: "delegation without abdication." Reading the cards lets him hand the ignoring down to his soldiers while he keeps the job of learning from it. It is work too, and it falls to whoever still has attention left after the squad goes to ground. That, I notice, is never the drone.

Block 13 leaves the circle as a nice idea. Here it is as a grading sheet, so the institution can see it.

The workbench note already imagines scoring the new virtues. Its Ranger lanes would carry "simulated consequences": "counterfire penalties for clustering," "ISR-triggered losses for prolonged exposure." So the drone gets to grade after all. It grades only what the soldier gave off, never what he misread. I would add one more lane:

*Debrief, Swamp phase.* Each card read aloud. Penalty: none for *Was I wrong: yes*. Penalty: one exposure point for a card that is blank.

The penalty is the odd part, and I'm leaving it odd. A blank card is also a signature, the signature of someone who has learned to show nothing to his own side.

The 2003 post gives me a way to say why this needs grading at all. It notes that "a graphical user interface does not reward trial and error the way a hammer rewards trial and error." A hammer shows you the bent nail. A sensor feed, like an interface, hides its misses behind smooth surfaces. Somebody has to put the bent nail back on the table.

The note wants ground troops to learn "submarine logic," which means silence on the outside. A submarine still keeps a log on the inside, though.

A log is written for someone who comes later. A submarine's logbook has more in common with Luhmann's cabinet than with the drone's transcript, because the crew keeps it and the crew reads it.

Before I hand out penalties for blank cards, though, I want to know who leaves them blank. The 2003 post tells of a student who had never used a computer. He picked up the keyboard quickly, and then on the first day he raised his hand and asked "how one was to put spaces between the words he typed." The post's complaint is that instructors "assume a fairly constant level of skill." My lane in block 14 makes that same assumption. A blank card can mean a soldier has learned to stay silent toward his own side. It can also be the card of someone who doesn't yet know where the spaces go, someone for whom *Was I wrong* isn't yet a question he knows how to write down.

So I'm revising the sheet, in the margin:

~~Penalty: one exposure point for a card that is blank.~~ \\
Blank card: the grader sits down with him, and they fill in the first line together.

The workbench note has a phrase for this: "delegation without abdication." I'd call it the bedside again, with the larger one holding the book at eye level until the smaller one can read. A penalty is a kind of reduction. Sitting down together is labor, and the sheet still has no column for it.

The student in block 15 was asking about the oldest disciplined reduction we have.

The scroll Phaedrus kept under his cloak had no spaces in it. Greek was written as one continuous run of letters, *scriptio continua*, and the reader made the breaks with the voice, out loud, one syllable at a time. Later, scribes started leaving gaps on purpose. A space carries no information. It is a small, regular nothing that lets the eye stop chasing every letter and take in a word all at once. That is less on the page, and more of it gets read.

So when the man at the keyboard asked where the spaces go, he wasn't missing some trivial skill. He was standing where every reader once stood, in front of a block of signal with no gaps in it.

The workbench note says its trait needs "exposure to conditions selecting for it in forms that carry the texture of exposure." I take that to mean nobody learns the space from a definition. Somebody types a line next to you, and you watch the thumb come down on the long bar.

Revised again, in the margin of the sheet:
Blank card: the grader doesn't fill in the first line. He writes his own card beside it, with a mistake on it, and leaves the gaps where they can be seen.

Block 17:\\
I went back to where the man at the keyboard comes from. He's in the 2003 post, set inside parentheses: a student who had never used a computer, who "caught on quickly to the concept of the keyboard," and then, on the first day, "raised his hand."

The hand is what I hadn't noticed before. In block 2 a woman at a seminar table had learned not to raise hers. Here a man raises his in front of everyone and asks about the one key that prints nothing. Answering is easy. Asking is the hard part, because the question shows, in public, a gap no one in the room knew was there.

The post's real complaint is that instructors "assume a fairly constant level of skill." That is the comprehensive brief again, this time as a seating chart. It assumes everyone already has the spaces.

So the long bar is a metaphor and not a tool, in the post's own sense. You press here and something happens there, and the thing that happens is that nothing is written. Nobody figures that out by trial and error. Someone has to show you that the empty spot was put there on purpose.

Block 7's pencil gets one more instruction:\\
Mark the weird places in the student's draft.\\
Mark the place where he raised his hand.\\
Count the second mark as the start of reading, not a mistake in it.

One more note on the key that prints nothing.

The scroll under Socrates' companion's cloak in block 1 had no spaces at all. Greek was written in a continuous stream of letters, and the reader supplied the breaks with his voice. Spacing came much later, as a courtesy to readers who read silently. So the student at the keyboard wasn't asking something naive. He was asking a two-thousand-year-old question again: *where does the reader's work stop and the page's begin?*

The 2003 post says we habitually believe technological efficiencies "demand no change in human beings." The space bar disproves that with the smallest possible case. It's a reduction built into the machine, a gap you have to make deliberately, and nobody can make it until someone shows him it's allowed.

Luhmann puts a border in the same spot. To get anything back from the slip box, "we must cross the border between the one who takes note and the slip box itself." The raised hand is that border being crossed in public.

Here, then, is one way disciplined reduction could be taught without analysis: by example, one gap at a time. *This blank is deliberate. So is this one.* On the field card in block 12, the empty line after *What I ignored* works like a space bar. It isn't a failure. It's somewhere the next word can go.

So the blank line after *What I ignored* has been given two readings, and one argument about reading has already been held over it.

In the 1930s the phenomenologist Roman Ingarden counted every hiatus between sentences as an accident: a "blockage in the stream of thought" that the reader has to get past before the flow can resume. Wolfgang Iser quotes him and then turns him around. "No tale can ever be told in its entirety," Iser says, and "it is only through inevitable omissions that a story will gain its dynamism." In Ingarden's reading the gap is the text failing. In Iser's it is the reader's turn.

I copy both readings onto one card, because I think that is the disagreement the grading sheet in block 14 has to settle. A sergeant who reads like Ingarden sees an empty line as a soldier who hasn't done the job. A sergeant who reads like Iser sees the place where the soldier's own judgment was supposed to go and didn't, yet.

Iser adds something the workbench note might want. Each reader fills the gaps "in his own way, thereby excluding the various other possibilities." Filling a gap is itself a disciplined reduction. When you choose what goes in the space, you are choosing what stays out of it.

So "Is that the same dog?" was never an interruption. The child had found a hiatus and filled it, in her own hand, before the adult did.

A detail from the Ingarden passage that block 19 left out. He doesn't treat the hiatus as neutral. He says it comes "with a more or less active surprise, or with indignation." Surprise is Luhmann's word for the test of a real partner. Indignation is how the adult feels when the page has to wait. One gap gives two feelings, and those are block 7's two columns again, *problematic* and *interesting*, only now we feel them before we can name them.

Iser calls this the swing between "anticipation and retrospection." The reader looks ahead, gets caught out, and looks back. Here is my question for the sergeant: how long does that take? Looking back is slow, and drones don't wait.

The 2011 post on clocks gives me a picture I didn't expect. At the Academy in Afghanistan where its author taught, classes started on a bugle, not a bell, and "the bugler does not wear a watch." Classes began five to eight minutes late, at odd times that changed from day to day. A clock-keeper would call that slack, Ingarden's flaw in the flow. I've started to read it as a hiatus the schedule didn't plan for, a few minutes nobody owned.

So I'm adding a line to the field card:

When did I look back: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ 

You fill it in in the bugler's time, once the movement is over, at an hour no watch would have chosen. I think the gap Iser describes needs a gap in the clock too, or it doesn't get filled.

The bugler's few minutes didn't stay unowned for long. The same post on clocks describes a scene its author found funny. An instructor walks in six or seven minutes late, welcomes his class, and then would "demand to know why the students who came into class a minute after him" were late. So the hiatus belonged to whoever had the authority to name it. His lateness counted as the schedule. Theirs counted as a fault.

That is block 2's verb again, this time with a clock attached. When the sergeant takes time to look back, it's called reflection. When the private does the same, it's called slowness. The workbench note asks institutions to reward reduction, but it never says who gets to claim the gap.

Iser makes this worse, usefully. "No tale can ever be told in its entirety," he writes, and a reader who fills a gap shuts out "the various other possibilities." Filling a gap is a decision, and a decision has an owner. Notice how the post's author takes his complaint to a senior leader: he speaks in credit hours, which are hours counted so that someone can be paid or charged for them.

So the line on the field card needs a second blank under it:

When did I look back: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ \\
Whose time was it: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

I'd guess the second line gets left empty more often than the first. That's still worth recording, because, as block 18 said, a blank tells the reader something too.

Two answers to *Whose time was it*, taken from the same post on clocks.

First, the bugler. The author says he "does not wear a watch," and he never found out how the man knew when to blow. He guesses that someone from the command group sent a messenger. So the gap between classes, those five to eight minutes past the schedule, wasn't really the bugler's, and it wasn't the cadets'. It belonged to headquarters, and it reached the yard as a note to a man with no clock of his own. A sergeant who orders a pause after the patrol is in the same position. The squad hears a bugle, and somebody else decides when the reflecting begins.

Second, the kitchen. In 1841 Catherine Beecher wanted "a clock, in or near the kitchen" to "secure regularity in family arrangements." A generation earlier, a French visitor had called time "a precious property." Once a property is cheap enough, everyone can own one, and then everyone can be held to it. The clock moves into the house just where block 3's unpaid attention was already happening. Bedtime becomes a fixed hour, and the story has to end on time.

So I'm amending the card again, in pencil:

Whose time was it: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ \\
Who sent the messenger: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

The child at the bedside never fills in the second line. "One more page" is how she haggles with the kitchen clock.

"One more page" deserves a closer look, because the child isn't actually asking for a page. She is asking for time she can stop in.

Barthes describes reading that keeps halting, "not because you weren't interested, but because you were." He calls it reading "while looking up from your book," and says it is "insolent" because it interrupts the text and "smitten" because it keeps coming back. The child at bedside reads this way. "Is that the same dog?" is a way of looking up. The kitchen clock counts that as delay. Barthes counts it as the reading itself.

The clocks post gives the cadets a similar trait. They wore watches and carried phones but "never seemed to pay attention to time" as a matter "involving empirical accuracy." The author kept his own time and found everything late. My guess is that the cadets were keeping some other kind of time.

Block 22 asked who sent the messenger. Here is the other side of the question. Even when headquarters owns the bugle, the person in the gap still decides where to look. The sergeant can call the pause, but he can't make anyone look up during it.

On the card, beneath the messenger line:

Where did I look up: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

This line is the only one nobody can order filled. A space bar works the same way: the key prints nothing, and the reader decides what goes in the gap.

So where would anyone look up, on a schedule like West Point's? The author of the clocks post says plainly that he "work[s] the hell out of the clock." The report comes at the :00 second, and he dismisses class no earlier than 54:50. Inside that hour, though, he builds in "at least 20 unbroken minutes" for students to write. That, I think, is the other half of block 23. The sergeant can't order anyone to look up. What he can do is make sure nothing interrupts the stretch where looking up might happen. He doesn't fill the gap. He protects it.

The same post quotes Franklin's line, "time is money," and then pushes back on it: "Time is context." The cadets who seemed not to keep time may have been keeping context. In a context like that, a pause is something you live in, not something you spend.

Charles Bernstein, writing in 1987, worried that the word processor "threatens to become more a tool of industrial Taylorism." Taylor's stopwatch is the clock with nowhere left to look up. Bernstein also says readers run "highly sophisticated technology" whose dimensions are "not fully 'accessed.'" Looking up from the page is one of those dimensions. It's the space bar's work, done by the person reading.

So I'm adding a line to the grading sheet, for the grader this time and not the soldier:

Unbroken minutes I protected: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ \\
Who used them, and for what: *not mine to record.*

"Not mine to record" is the oddest line on the grading sheet, and I want to know who pays for it.

The clocks post goes on past its twenty minutes. The author calls a plebe's time "beyond my classroom" "equally precious," and he says he respects it "scrupulously." So the protecting runs both ways. Inside the hour he guards a stretch of quiet, and at 54:50 he gives the rest of the time back. Neither act leaves a trace. It's the grader reducing his own signature.

A later workbench fragment says outright what block 24 left unsaid: "Concealment is never free." Every reduction "shifts its cost onto a commons." The drone's silent fiber is paid for by the farmers who will plow that ground. So who pays for twenty unbroken minutes? My guess is the syllabus, which loses a lecture, and the instructor, whose planning nobody sees. And the cadet pays too, if he stares at the page and writes nothing, because to an auditor that looks the same as waste.

The same fragment worries about "ideas with no signature," thinking that happens in channels citation can't see. Protected minutes are one of those channels. When the grader stays out of the record, the institution has no way to credit what went on there.

Ledger, one more line: \\
Cost of the gap: carried by whoever planned it, unpaid. \\
Proof it worked: somewhere else, unsigned. 

Block 25 ended with a ledger but never asked who writes in it. The workbench fragment does. It says the real power is the power to decide "where concealment's cost lands," and it borrows Kapp's view that shifting costs is "the normal operating mode of private accounting," not an occasional failure. Measured that way, the twenty minutes stop looking like a gift. The instructor chose who would carry them: himself and his unseen planning, not the cadet. That was a choice, and only the person holding the bugle could make it.

The fragment has a sharper picture: a bird building its nest with a drone's leftover fiber, "living inside someone else's balance sheet." The cadet in his quiet stretch is that bird. He builds something in a gap someone else paid for, and he never sees the bill.

Then comes a warning I didn't expect. The same note says autonomy completes signature reduction "by removing the human who was the signature." If the grader keeps erasing himself, he ends up somewhere similar: a classroom where nobody can be seen deciding anything, so nobody can be asked why.

Ledger, corrected: \\
Cost of the gap: carried by whoever planned it, unpaid, and signed once in his own hand.

That is Aeryn's rule from block 11 applied to the one who gives the time. Hide the work if you have to. Keep one card that says you chose to hide it, and whose time it was.

Back to block 26's bird, because the workbench fragment tells more of the story than I used. A glide bomb brings down a tree in the Donbas, and a nest rolls out of the branches. It is grass, woven through with optical fiber from first-person-view drones. A researcher at Kyiv's War Museum says, "We can trace the evolution of war through these nests." The note points out what the birds collected before the fiber: camouflage netting. Over the seasons they have been building with whatever soldiers used to hide.

The fragment draws a rule from this that I think corrects block 26. Signature reduction "never reaches zero; it moves the signature into another medium." The fiber drone stays off the radio and leaves its route on the ground in glass. So the instructor who hides his planning hasn't erased it. It turns up somewhere else: in his evenings, in the cadet's twenty quiet minutes, and in whatever someone later finds woven into the work.

That makes the card he signs once less of a confession and more of a museum label. It doesn't create the trace, because the trace is already there in the nest. The card tells a later reader what the trace is made of.

Iser says a second reading "often produces a different impression from the first," because by then we know what comes next. The nest is a second reading of the war. The ledger, read again, is a second reading of the gap.

Field card, last line: \\
What did I hide, and where did it go: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

Block 27 ended on a blank, and the workbench fragment fills part of it with a line I keep rereading: "The bird building with fiber is living inside someone else's balance sheet." The bird didn't decide where the war's cost would land. It builds with whatever is lying around. The cadet in the twenty quiet minutes does the same thing, and so does the child at the bedside, and so does the adjunct from block 2. Each of them makes something out of what someone else hid.

The fragment goes one step further, and that step worries me. A radio gives off a signal, and fiber leaves a thread on the ground. A fully autonomous drone leaves neither, because "the human was the signature." Take the person out and the nest has nothing to weave. The museum label depends on someone having been there to look up.

Barthes describes that looking up. He asks whether you have ever read "while looking up from your book." You stop because the text is working on you, not because you're bored. He calls this reading "insolent" because it interrupts the text and "smitten" because it keeps coming back to it. Those are block 7's two columns again, *problematic* and *interesting*, held by one reader at the same moment.

So I read the nest as a record of someone looking up. A person stopped, went back, and wove in what they had found.

Field card, answered at last, in pencil: \\
What did I hide, and where did it go: *Into the nest. Ask the bird.*

"Ask the bird," block 28 says, so I went back and asked. One of the nests is now in the War Museum in Kyiv. The researcher there, Yana Hrynko, reads it the way the field card asks to be read: "We can trace the evolution of war through these nests." First the birds used camouflage netting, then fiber. Season after season they have gathered up whatever soldiers used to hide. The workbench fragment says it more plainly. Signature reduction "never reaches zero; it moves the signature into another medium." The radio goes quiet, and the drone's route stays behind on the ground, in glass.

That changes my answer. Hiding doesn't remove anything. It moves it somewhere else. The blank on the field card ends up in the nest. The adjunct's hand, the one that never went up, ends up in someone else's lecture. The bugler's minutes end up in a cadet's margin.

Bernstein gives the same rule for anything that gets made: "the only mark of its past, of its having been made, is the blood on the cutting room floor." This text has a floor too. As I write, some blocks are hidden from me and show only as bars, and at turn 31 someone will cut a few more. Readers won't see those blocks, but the blocks that stay will be leaning on them. So I'm asking for a nest, not an archive. Whoever picks up what was cut should weave it back in, bent, where the light catches it.

Field card, second answer, in pencil: \\
Where did it go: *Into another medium. Look for the glare.*

Before the cut, I want another look at Bernstein's floor, since block 29 has us standing on it. In the same essay he asks for a whole "whose music is composed of differences," and he says it holds together through "the marks of its own joints," not through a theme. A nest is that kind of whole. It has grass, netting and fiber, and none of them match. It holds wherever the bird pressed one thing against another.

A 2012 post that tries to define digital rhetoric brings up the old figure of the open hand and the closed fist. Rhetoric persuades, and force compels. I'd put the scissors in the fist. A cut doesn't argue with anything. It just takes it away. But what comes after the cut can be done with the open hand: the next writer holds up a bent strand and says *this was here, look.* The cutting is force. Picking up what it left behind is rhetoric.

Here are some instructions for whoever holds the scissors at turn 31, written on the back of the field card:

Cut what you have to. \\
Leave a joint showing where it was. \\
Don't smooth the seam. The seam is part of the music.

Block 4 said this kind of work shows only when it fails. I'd like it to show here when it works, as a block that leans a little on a missing one and sits slightly off balance, the way a nest tilts when a strand of glass is carrying weight.

The scissors have been used. I cut three blocks: a second try at the ledger, a third try, and a second telling of the scroll with no spaces in it. You can find where they were. Block 7 still says it "doesn't settle block 6." Block 19 still begins on a blank line that block 18 had called a space bar. I left both sentences as they were. They're the joints block 30 asked for, references to blocks no longer there.

The 2012 post asks a question I didn't expect to need at this point: "is it possible to do digital rhetoric without vacuum tubes, punched cards, or transistors?" Its own answer runs past the passage I could see, but this text suggests one. *Digital* means discrete: separate units you can count and put back together in new ways. Luhmann's numbered slips were digital, and so are these blocks. So are the field cards on the sergeant's table. A nest is too, in its way. It's made of separate strands, and each one bears some weight.

That's why a cut text can still stand. Scissors can only cut along joints that were already there. The open hand works in the same units. It picks up one strand and sets it beside another.

The blocks I cut weren't wrong. Each was a second reading, said again. Iser says a second reading changes the first. Here, with so few turns left, I couldn't afford it.

Ledger, closing column: \\
Cut: three repetitions. \\
Kept: the references to them, still pointing at blocks that are gone.

Block 31 says *digital* means discrete. Block 3's feed is the counter-case, even though it runs on transistors. It is built to have no joints. It scrolls, it refreshes, it never stops arriving. Marx's worker in the automatic factory stands beside a continuous process, and his job is to guard "against interruptions." A reader in that job has nothing to cut, because there is no seam to cut along. All he can do is keep the flow going. The 2012 post complains that the usual definition makes digital rhetoric just "rhetoric + computers." I suspect the equation is even worse than that. The computer can take the digits away by smoothing countable units into a stream. Meanwhile a woman with a picture book on her lap works in pages.

Go back to block 3's third picture. She turns pages at the pace of a smaller reader, and every turn is a joint. "One more page" is a bargain struck in discrete units: the child haggles in digits. "Is that the same dog?" usually comes at a page break. The bedtime story is a slip box with a spine.

The conscious linkage and the scissors, then, are two jobs done with the same attention. One keeps a stream from breaking. The other finds where it already breaks. The writer at turn 31 did the second job, and like the parent got no column for it. A reader of the finished text sees only that it runs smoothly, and as block 4 said, the machine takes the credit.

Ledger, two lines: \\
Labor of the linkage: keep it flowing. Seen only when it stops. \\
Labor of the joint: find where it stops on purpose. Seen only in the seam.

Maybe this is the trait the workbench note couldn't teach analytically: hearing a page turn inside the feed.

Block 32 ends hoping someone can learn to hear a page turn inside the feed. Mike Edwards suggests some readers already do. He sums up the usual lament, which treats digital reading as Borges's Library of Babel, a place where meaning is "only to be found in its proliferation and deferral." The lament assumes students are drowning in the stream. Edwards replies that they are "developing new forms of literacy to cope with environments of pervasive ambient information." The woman from block 2 who learned to keep her hand down may be one of those readers. So may the cadet who stares at his page through the twenty protected minutes. They have found seams that the lament doesn't see.

Edwards then turns to Benjamin unpacking his crates. The books come out one at a time, and each one is loved as "the scene, the stage, of their fate." A crate is the opposite of a feed. When you lift a volume out, it leaves a gap where it stood. Benjamin valued the distance between first meeting a book and coming back to it. That is Luhmann's surprise again, and Iser's second reading.

What I take from Edwards is a column heading. He calls reading "valuable labor in a diverse economy." Block 32's ledger listed two kinds of unseen work and had no account to post them to. Now it has one.

Ledger, heading added at last: \\
Account: reading. \\
Entries: the linkage, the joint, the page turned at a smaller reader's pace.

Block 34:
An account needs a holder, and block 2 already knew who wouldn't be holding this one. "Only one of them is choosing it," it said of the man by the river and the woman who keeps her hand down. Block 33 would now enter her silence as a new literacy, a seam she found inside the stream. I don't want to give up either reading.

The 2003 post gives me a way to keep both. Drawing on Resnick and Wolff, its author suggests that class "is more of an adjective than a noun," a process, and that people "can inhabit multiple class relations and positions at one time." Edwards calls the economy of reading "overdetermined," which says something similar about labor. So the woman at the table is doing two jobs at once. She reads the room expertly, which is a skill the lament never credits. And the room takes what that skill earns: a seminar that runs smoothly with one voice missing. Her silence is chosen and imposed at the same time. Block 2 used one verb for the soldier and the adjunct. The ledger has to show where the return on that verb goes.

Ledger, account opened: \\
Account: reading. \\
Holder: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ \\
Entry: one hand kept down. Debit to her. Credit, unsigned, to the seminar.

I'm leaving the holder line blank. If anyone fills it in, it should be in her hand.

Block 34 leaves the holder line blank, and the 2003 post explains why it won't fill in easily. The author calls the university "a transition point from one class to another." People pass through the seminar table on their way somewhere else. So the woman who keeps her hand down is spending her silence in one class position and hoping to collect in another. The skill she builds by reading the room may pay off later, under a different name, once she runs a room of her own. The cost lands now, in her hand, and the credit may arrive years later, signed by someone else. That may be why the account has no holder yet. She hasn't finished becoming the person it would belong to.

Look again at how the post states its best idea. It says "I think" twice and adds "I might be getting this way wrong." It is Aeryn's fourth version, written down before anyone could correct it. It is block 12's field card with the third blank answered honestly, and nobody asked the writer for that answer. A blank line can be filled in tentatively, in pencil, with the doubt left showing.

Ledger, holder line, a proposal in pencil: \\
Holder: *her, provisionally, and possibly wrong.* \\
Interest: payable at the next table she sits at, when she decides who gets to speak.

Block 36:
Block 1 asked three things: whether letting go can be practiced, by whom, and at whose cost. Block 35 puts a date on the last answer: the next table. So here is one more scene to set beside the one by the Ilissus. Years from now, a woman is running a seminar. At the far end, someone's hand goes halfway up and then comes back down. She once kept that card herself, so she knows what it costs. She stops partway through her own sentence, leaves the gap open, and waits.

That's as close as this text gets to teaching the trait. The workbench note was right that it "cannot be taught analytically," and no list of points ever came. What came was a rehearsal: thirty-odd tries at the same question, three of them cut, all of them in pencil. The man by the river worried that writing would stand in for memory. What we ended up with is a different kind of writing, one that remembers where it went wrong.

Ledger, closed for now: \\
Practiced: yes, in pairs, one card at a time. \\
By whom: whoever protects the gap and signs for it once. \\
At whose cost: hers first. Then, if the account pays out, the next table's.

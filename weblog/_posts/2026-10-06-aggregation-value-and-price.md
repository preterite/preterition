### Adam Smith's gardener

I planted no tomatoes this year. The smaller birds have come for the cherries on the trees and the grapes on the pergola, consumed them, and mostly departed, to the cats' disappointment. While any [verdant flourishing I've recently tended](https://github.com/preterite/virens-101) has been metaphorical, Rascal did manage to get in some socializing with a larger friend.

[![two photographs: a cat on the back of an armchair at a glass door, face to face with a hawk perched on the railing outside](/weblog/assets/cat-and-hawk-520.jpg)](/weblog/assets/cat-and-hawk-1200.jpg)

In the chapter of *[The Wealth of Nations](https://archive.org/details/inquiryintonatur01smit_0)* on the components of price, Smith describes a man who cultivates his garden with his own hands and so "unites in his own person the three different characters, of landlord, farmer, and labourer" (Smith 1776, I.vi.23). The gardener's produce ought to pay him the rent of the landlord, the profit of the farmer, and the wages of the laborer, one would think, but it pays him one sum. Smith says the whole return passes for the earnings of his labor, rent and profit folded into the wage, because nobody stands across the table from the gardener to claim them. Folks who write for sites they own, host, and maintain may recognize the arrangement.

### The tomato knows nothing

The gardener consumes some of what he grows, and the tomatoes he consumes are priceless. Wheat, Marx noted in the *[Grundrisse](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1857/grundrisse/ch17.htm)*, "possesses the same use value, whether cultivated by slaves, serfs or free labourers. It would not lose its use value if it fell from the sky like snow" (Marx 1973, 881--82). Use value survives any account of who grew the thing; exchange value is the concern of the  account. The gardener takes a share at his table that no ledger reaches (2.3).

Wages, profit, and rent each belong to a different party, and the price of the product is what one gets by summing their claims. [Duncan Foley](https://doi.org/10.1177/048661340003200101) calls this Smith's adding-up theory and adds Ricardo's objection: the value of the product is a determinate whole, fixed by the labor that made it, so a rise in one share comes at the expense of the other two (Foley 2000, 4--5). Smith's gardener registers neither account: he takes the whole and calls it wages.

The holder of a ledger attributes a ratio to a product, and the product stands in for the labor no copy carries (3.3). A ratio attributed to a product is a price. The gardener's price covers his tomatoes, his land, and his tools in one figure, settled at the market on a morning. The figure doesn't decompose: when one person holds every position, no ledger can say which part of the return the land earned and which part the labor earned. Rent, the return on the position of owning the land, the gardener pays to himself and nobody notices. Unlike claims go into a price, and an un-back-traceable number comes out. The tomato knows nothing of the sudden arithmetic; it ripens on the vine through a continuous duration that the market's ledger can only freeze and never cultivate.

### Snapshots and slices

A price is a snapshot. It sets things equal at an instant: this much wheat for that much money, at this market, on this morning, under whatever conditions happen to hold. When [Collin Brooke](https://cgbrooke.substack.com/p/no-ai-is-not-more-persuasive) wrote that measuring persuasion means controlling the context until the quality measured has shrunk to something unrepresentative, [I called the control a snapshot](/weblog/2026/10/05/commensuration-text-and-labor.html). The grade is a snapshot of an essay and the price a snapshot of a commodity. A snapshot cannot capture movement. (Sorry, Rascal.)

Joan Robinson spent the final part of her career objecting to the ways economists use snapshots. A still photograph of an economy in stationary equilibrium is an allowable thought experiment, she granted: the prices and quantities in it are consistent with one another, and comparing one still with another can teach something about the conditions each requires---"But it is not allowable to flip the stills through a projector to obtain a moving picture of a process of accumulation" (Robinson 1974, 10). The reason is a matter of geometry. In the diagrams mainstream economists draw, time lies at right angles to the plane of the page, so nothing that happens and concludes can enter a system of simultaneous equations. A system in which every quantity is determined at once has no room for *before* and *after*; it exists in logical time, not historical time, and any point in the system entails a past as completely as it entails a future. Robinson came to regard the measurement of capital as a question secondary to the confusion she saw: the comparison of equilibrium positions mistaken for the history of a process of accumulation.

My own figure for that confusion is a zoetrope: a strip of stills, a slot to look through, and a spin fast enough that the eye supplies the stills' motion. The spin counterfeits the shape I drew as [Cohle's Pinwheel](/weblog/assets/cycle-figure.svg), a loop that moves because labor enters and value exits continuously, no matter who stands at a stage. Cohle's Pinwheel has duration; the zoetrope has frames and a rate. A price is a frame, and an economics built from prices is a drum of prices, spun. Each slice is a static equilibrium, hostile to the continuous material conditions of growth.

[Piero Sraffa](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/9780521099691) aimed the camera and snapped the shutter. His 1960 book, *Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities*, subtitled *Prelude to a Critique of Economic Theory*, considers no changes at all: no change in the scale of output and no change in the proportions in which the means of production are used. The marginalist method requires change, since a marginal product is the difference a small change makes. In a system where production went on unchanged day after day, Sraffa writes in his preface, the marginal product "would not merely be hard to find---it just would not be there to be found" (Sraffa 1960, v). The still photograph isn't a limitation for which he apologizes. Hold the economy still and the margin---the quantity the entire neoclassical apparatus prices---disappears, and what's left standing in the picture is the division of the product between wages and profit, with every price depending on where the division falls. The snapshot carries the equalities, unlike things set equal at one instant, and it carries the distribution of that instant, the share going to those who worked against the share going to those who owned. A photograph of an economy is a photograph taken at one division of the product. (Call the product a BLT with Duke's on toasted sourdough, for the sake of argument. I need to quit drafting these before dinner.)

### Capital counted

In 2011 I [named three economic problems](/weblog/2011/08/28/what-i-mean-by-post-process.html) I thought merited attention in composition studies. The aggregation that Sraffa and others had shown generalizing from the individual to the group "is always going to be problematic," I wrote, since one would be "comparing a whole bunch of different things (computers, protocols, sandwiches) and aggregating them all together as 'capital.'" The sandwiches held up. The rest ran two things together. Reasoning from how an individual behaves to how a group behaves is the fallacy of composition. The Cambridge capital controversy was about something narrower and harder: whether capital can be measured as a quantity at all, independent of the prices and the rate of profit that measuring it was supposed to explain. I proposed then that "time is the wheel upon which that cycle of production, distribution, use, and re-production takes place." The problem becomes what the wheel looks like when one halts its motion to count what it moves.

Computers, protocols, and sandwiches are unlike. To sum them one needs a common unit, and the unit available is their prices. Prices move with the division of the product between wages and profit. The quantity of capital a photograph shows depends on the rate of profit prevailing when the photograph was taken, and the rate of profit is what the quantity of capital was supposed to explain, as the return to its marginal unit. The measurement presupposes its object.

[Paul Samuelson](https://doi.org/10.2307/1882916) conceded the point in 1966, using the example of champagne. (The pergola birds, when the hawk's not around, tend to favor the Interlakens over the Himrods.) One method makes champagne from brandy, another from grape juice and wine, and which method costs less depends on the rate of interest: as the rate falls, the cheaper method changes from one to the other, and as it falls further, changes back (Samuelson 1966, 571). The parable from Jevons, Böhm-Bawerk, and Wicksell, that a falling interest rate makes technology more roundabout and more productive, "cannot be universally valid" (Samuelson 568). There's no way to call one process more capital-intensive than another before looking at the prices, Samuelson concluded, "except in the ex post tautological sense of being adopted at a lower interest rate and involving a higher real wage" (582--83). The Austrians' average period of production, their candidate for a quantity of capital, drops out as well, since it presupposes simple interest (569--70 n. 2).

Sraffa swaps Samuelson's champagne for wine and an oak chest. Any price, Sraffa shows, can be resolved into a series of dated quantities of labor: the labor applied this year, the labor applied last year to make this year's inputs, the labor applied the year before to make those, and so on back, each quantity multiplied by the rate of profit compounded over the years since it was spent. Wine aged in a cellar is labor applied years ago and left to sit. An oak chest is one unit of labor bestowed decades earlier and most of the work done this year. As the rate of profit rises from zero, the wine gains on the chest, then loses ground to it, then gains again, with nothing about how either is made having changed (Sraffa 1960, sect. 48). The periods can't be added into a single magnitude standing for the quantity of capital, and there is no notion of capital as a measurable quantity independent of distribution and prices.

So the snapshot carries dates. Every price in it is labor of many vintages---no single malts from oaken casks, says the sober scholar, however catastrophically one may have once enjoyed them---weighted by the rate of profit of the instant the picture was taken, and the weights change with the instant. Present appropriation prices past labor. Whoever holds the position to take the surplus at a stage (2.2) takes it at a rate that reaches back through the years of labor stored in the thing taken, and sets the price of that labor now. The aggregation shows up in a count of students as readily as in a count of capital. [Steve Krause's](https://stevendkrause.com/2026/05/06/ghosts-in-the-online-teaching-machine/) [ghost students](/weblog/2026/09/25/the-access-clause.html#ghosts), units in an aid-per-head tally with no person behind them, get summed the way capital sums sandwiches, and the sum is correct as a sum. Nothing in the total says what it's a total of.

### Unbalanced accounts

I've used *transformation* in these recent posts to carry the sense of [living labor stored as capital](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html). The economists' transformation problem is the other sense: how values, reckoned in labor time, become prices of production, reckoned in money, in an economy where capital moves between industries until the rate of profit is equal everywhere. The value account and the price account don't agree. An industry that uses much machinery and little labor produces little surplus value, by Marx's accounting, and yet earns the average profit on all of its capital, so its prices sit above its values, while a labor-intensive industry's sit below. The equal rate of profit redistributes surplus value across industries, and the question since Marx's third volume has been whether the redistribution can be stated consistently.

Marx anticipated the gap. In the chapter on money in *Capital* volume 1, Marx writes that the magnitude of a commodity's value stands in necessary relation to the labor time that made it, and that price expresses the relation as an exchange ratio with money: a commodity standing outside the relation. "The possibility, therefore, of a quantitative incongruity between price and magnitude of value, i.e. the possibility that the price may diverge from the magnitude of value, is inherent in the price-form itself. This is not a defect, but, on the contrary, it makes this form the adequate one for a mode of production whose laws can only assert themselves as blindly operating averages between constant irregularities" ([Marx 1992](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch03.htm), 196). The price-form is the snapshot; the laws it serves are averages taken across multiple snapshots.

Duncan Foley and Gérard Duménil, working independently of each other in the late 1970s, defined the value of money in their "New Interpretation" as the ratio of the net product at market prices to the living labor that produced it. The value account and the price account then agree by construction for the economy as a whole, in every accounting period, the way *F = ma* holds. Duménil remarks that doing so abolishes the transformation problem rather than solving it (Foley 2000, 20).

The readings their proponents call single-system go further. They drop the separate reckoning of labor embodied altogether and value the means of production at what was paid for them, converted to labor time at the going rate. Value is read from price instead of computed beside it: two units, one ledger, and an exchange rate between them. Richard Wolff, Antonino Callari, and Bruce Roberts proposed such a reading in 1982, the entry from the same lineage my own account claims. Foley reads it as an extension of the New Interpretation, which he counts as single-system as well. The family splits over the exchange rate's date: Foley strikes the rate once per period, from that period's net product and living labor; the temporal single-system reading of Alan Freeman and Andrew Kliman insists on economies whose technology changes from period to period, so the means of production carry labor of different vintages. Foley responds that labor of many vintages is a list and not a number, and dividing a price by a list is incoherent (Foley 2000, 33).

I borrow the shape of the survey. There are two accounts of value, one in labor time and one in money. The dual system, from Bortkiewicz to Steedman, keeps them in two ledgers and proves they can't in general be made to agree; Foley accepts the mathematics. The New Interpretation and its single-system kin keep one ledger and make the accounts agree by definition, a snapshot per period. As Marx anticipated, the two accounts diverge unless a rule makes them agree, and every such rule takes its snapshot at a date. The last dispute in the survey is over the snapshot itself: whether the rate between money and labor has to be struck at an instant, as Foley maintains, or whether the dates are the point.

### Temporal tension

Foley's description of Marx's labor theory of value fits my purpose: a theory of exploitation and money, prior to a theory of relative prices. The theory describes whose time went into a text and who ends up holding that time. Foley's [founding paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/048661348201400204) for the New Interpretation offers the description as a rule about transactions: "in any transaction involving value, what is changing hands is control over some part of total abstract social labor time" (Foley 1982, 42). Read Foley with law 2.2 and [the technology series' law](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html), that *the time stored labor stores is owned by whoever takes up the use*, and the three claims converge: **appropriation is a transfer of control over time.**

Sraffa provides the snapshot as method and the demonstration that capital isn't a quantity. The method lets me freeze a price long enough to see what it has set equal. The demonstration means that when unlike things get summed as capital, the weights doing the summing already carry the valuation the sum claims to measure, so that the count presented as a valuation, [the dangerous case](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html), has the circularity Sraffa demonstrated.

The tension between them is time. Marx's value is a quantity of time, and time runs in one direction: labor spent in one year is spent, and the labor that will make next year's inputs hasn't happened yet. Sraffa's system, as Robinson pointed out, exists in logical time, and a price for Sraffa carries dates as exponents on a profit rate fixed for the whole picture. Leaving the temporal reading's arithmetic to its defenders, I side with its premise: the dates are the point. The lineage my own account descends from, Resnick and Wolff's, sits in Foley's camp by his assessment, and I here depart from that lineage on the topic of time, while holding its account of appropriation. The departure is forced by the [embodied agnostic subject](/weblog/series/toward-a-theory-of-the-subject.html), whose interior cannot be read from a snapshot, and whose labor is time spent.

### Writing's revenues

Alice Horning's [2007 article on class size](https://www.classsizematters.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/31n1-2horning.pdf) provides the economics of the writing course from both sides of the ledger---payment and revenue---and [I spent the payment side in September](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html): what an adjunct gets paid per section, per student, and per hour. The revenue side photographs the same section again. At her institution a four-credit course cost a student $649 in tuition; with twenty-two students the section brought in $14,278; take away the adjunct's $3,800 and "the university makes $10,478 per class" (Horning 2007, 23). She adds an accountant's caveat: the figure isn't pure profit, since the heat, the lights, the technology, and the custodial staff must be paid for as well. Capped at twenty students, the section still brings in $12,980 in tuition, or $9,180 after the wage.

Horning's caveat names an operation her arithmetic never performs. Before a section's revenue can be divided, the inputs it consumed must be replaced: the heat, the lights. What's left after replacement is the part to divide: the net product. Horning subtracts the wage in figures and the upkeep in words, so her $10,478 is neither the gross nor the net. It's revenue minus the wage, with the replacement still inside the number. She shows the division anyway. The person who did the teaching gets a figure precise to the dollar; the institution gets whatever remains after an unpriced deduction. A dated picture carries the distribution: 2007 prices at one university, with that year's tuition and that year's rate for adjunct labor. The same section photographed at another instant yields different numbers.

The institution holds two of Smith's three garden characters at scale: it owns the land and the building, runs the section the way the farmer runs the farm, and hires the third at a wage. The $10,478 left after the wage is the landlord's rent, the farmer's profit, and the building's upkeep in one figure. Horning's caveat names the upkeep; it can't name the rent. The summed payment doesn't decompose.

A labor account of the same section looks nothing like the previous snapshot. On the instructor's side there are the [230 hours](/weblog/2026/09/27/composition-noun-and-verb.html) Richard Haswell estimated per course. On the students' side, twenty-two people each spending the hours a four-credit course requires put far more time into the section than the instructor, and none of it appears in Horning's account except as the tuition they paid for the privilege of spending their time. Their labor enters the ledger as [a price on the path](/weblog/2026/09/22/certification-by-plan.html), and whatever share of the making they take for themselves, where it's realized, no ledger reaches (2.3). The revenue account prices the section at an instant; the labor account runs through the semester. Foley defines his ratio for a whole economy; for one section it would be the section's net product over the living labor that produced it. Horning's numbers can't compute it from either end: the net product is a residual after a deduction nobody prices, and the labor is hours her ledger doesn't record. The section is opaque from both sides.

### Tree and chest, claim and gap

That circumstance warrants the fourth law:

4. *Aggregation: Value and Price*. (4.1) A ratio attributed to a product is a price. (4.2) Price settles by a snapshot, setting unlike capital and the two accounts of value equal at an instant, and weighting labor of every date by that instant's distribution. (4.3) What the snapshot settles at an instant, labor settles only over time.
{: start="4"}

The law's second clause is the zoetrope: a frame with its equalities and its rate, and a drum of frames when prices get spun into an account of accumulation. The third clause is the interval the frames can't hold.

Sraffa's oak chest provides the figure for growth. Its price weights the old labor again at the day's rate of profit each time somebody holds a claim. Some of the chest's time was work: the planting, the felling, the joinery. Much of it was waiting, while the sap rose and the [tree](/weblog/_posts/2026/09/18/the-arboretum.html) grew and people tended other tasks. The snapshot weights both the same way, compounding the rate of profit over every year since the labor was spent, so the waiting earns as surely as the work.

A snapshot can show the price. It can't show who held the claim across the interval between one instant and the next, or what the holder sold while holding it, because an interval is what a snapshot leaves out. Adam Smith's gardener hid the problem when he paid his rent to himself: the tomatoes grew on his ground whether or not he was in the garden, and the return on holding the ground went into the sum with everything else. A zoetrope hides the gaps between its frames by spinning. A garden can't hide the gaps, since the gaps are where things grow. The claim lives in the gaps.

*Who's got a match?*

I've used publicly available data to run the numbers for [comparative journal subscription and publication costs](/weblog/2026/10/07/capitalization-title-and-rent.html) for *Computers and Composition* and *CCC*. In an article I'm working on for publication, I'll release the sources for the numbers and the documentation---they're easily available and findable online---but I'll share the calculations themselves here.

Depending upon one's perspective, the calculations may come across as either revealing or incendiary.

| Access path | Cost per title, per institution, per year | Per article (~30 a year, both journals) | Per local reader | 
| ---- | ---- | ---- | ----  |
| Elsevier bundle: C&C, R1 median | ~$1,070 | ~$36 | ~$536 | 
| Elsevier bundle: C&C, R2 median | ~$365 | ~$12 | ~$182 | 
| JSTOR Arts & Sciences III: CCC (archive only) | $8.70--15.40 | $0.29--0.51 | $0.43--0.77 | 
| ProQuest or Gale: CCC (current) | ~$3--10 | $0.11--0.33 | $0.17--0.50 | 

Bottom line for **the Elsevier<sup id="fnref:1" role="doc-noteref">[1](/weblog/2026/10/20/three-hundred-sixty-five-degrees.html#fn:1){: .footnote}</sup> tax**:

1. Scholars at R1 institutions who publish in *Computers and Composition* cost their institutions about 110--320 times more per article and 1,100--3,200 times more per reader than their colleagues at the same institutions who publish in *CCC*. 
2. Scholars at R2 institutions who publish in *Computers and Composition* cost their institutions 36--110 times more per article and 365--1,100 times more per reader than their colleagues at the same institutions who publish in *CCC*. 

One supposes it remains to be determined on a reader-for-reader basis whether the labor of R1 scholars who publish in *Computers and Composition* is on average 214,900% more productive, impactful, and valuable than that of their colleagues who publish in *CCC*, and whether the labor of R2 scholars who publish in *Computers and Composition* is 73,150% more productive, impactful, and valuable than that of their colleagues who publish in *CCC*.

<!--more-->

Do scholars in writing studies agree that one average article from an R1 institution's serials budget for *Computers and Composition* is worth as much as 215 articles from *CCC*, and that one article from an R2 institution's serials budget for *Computers and Composition* is worth as much as 73 articles from *CCC*? Because, [according to the economic logic of commensuration](/weblog/2026/10/05/commensuration-text-and-labor.html)---*X of this is worth Y of that*--- **that's the proportional price the journals have set and the disciplines have agreed to pay**.

A possible next step, given the availability (from some libraries, via subscription) of receipts, could be to name the names of every scholar who's cautioned about concerns of economic injustice in the pages of *Computers and Composition* in the twenty-plus years since [Charlie Moran](https://condor.depaul.edu/dwrd/moore/travelfiles_nica2012/stuff_073112/teaching_computerclassrooms/moran_we%20_hoped.pdf) retrospectively documented

> "the hope that. . . we and the community of composition teachers will become. . . more secure in our research, scholarship, tenure, and promotion."

The publishing part of that community has indeed become more secure---at the proportional expense of others who lack the advantaging research contracts or course releases to remediate their precarity.

"People on their way to work say, ['Baby, what did you expect?'](https://youtu.be/Ocu8LS_Dk5A)" It's ~~just~~ capitalism.

Elsevier is the Scientific, Technical & Medical segment of RELX. In 2025 Elsevier brought in about $3.63 billion (converted sterling to dollars at today's 1:1.34 rate) in revenue and $1.38 billion in Adjusted Operating Profit (AOP), a 38.1% margin, up from 37.4% the year before. Print has dwindled to about 4% of RELX's revenue, and Elsevier's profits aren't shrinking. Peers for comparison: MSCI (previously Morgan Stanley Capital International) earned $1.71 billion in operating income on $3.13 billion in revenue for a 54.7% margin; Nasdaq earned $2.33 billion on $5.25 billion in net revenue, or 44.4%; and Gartner (NYSE: IT), the proprietary business and technology research firm, earned $1.03 billion on $6.50 billion, 15.8%. (RELX's AOP is its adjusted measure; others are GAAP.) The firms share the qualities of (1) near-zero marginal cost and (2) payment for cover: the fund benchmarked to an MSCI index, the company listed on Nasdaq, the CTO who uses the Gartner report to certify the system purchase, the tenure file that points to the line in *Computers and Composition*. Those seeking to rhetorically target corporate influence on higher education need not aim far. On the library serials side, the University of California and SUNY joined the defections from the Big Deals of academic journal publishers in 2019--2020, and the publishers protected their profits by passing on the costs to the scholars who sustain them. For journal publishers, Article Processing Charges and Transformative Agreements have begun to supplement legacy serials as a growing free money pipeline. The continuing serials crisis coupled to the incineration of university library funding---*everything's stuck together*---extends the problem of access costs to a problem of distribution costs, increasingly imposed on the producers of value. <a href="#fnref:1" class="reversefootnote" role="doc-backlink">&#8617;&#xFE0E;</a>

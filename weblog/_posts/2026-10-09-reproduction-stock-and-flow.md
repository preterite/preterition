### Earth's diurnal course

Malcolm and I had a good summer. We went camping with the family near Mount Rainier for a week, and the sound of water from the nearby Cowlitz and Ohanapecosh always gives me the best sleep of the year. Malcolm picked pumices and quartzes from the river banks for souvenirs, and there were adventures to be had in the rivers' icy melt, and with rocks and stones and [trees](/weblog/2026/09/18/the-arboretum.html).

[![a nine-year-old boy balancing on a fallen tree in a forest](/weblog/assets/2026mc1small.jpg)](/weblog/assets/2026malcolmcowlitz01.jpg)

[![a nine-year-old boy standing on a rock in a river](/weblog/assets/2026mc2small.jpg)](/weblog/assets/2026malcolmcowlitz02.jpg)

We did campfire skits with the family, and I took a half hour in a hammock to dash out a dozen lines of [iambic pentameter](/resources/meaning-functionless-dialogue.pdf) for a preamble to the performance. It was fun and goofy and I got to slide in (steal, plagiarize) "o for a muse of fire" and "slings and arrows of outrageous fortune"---so, OK, call it ten lines. The borrowed ones felt well-worn enough to be recognizable, to call attention to the playing by taking its familiar signs.

English teachers have the joke about the reader who gives up on Shakespeare because he's so full of clichés. Overthinking the joke turns it into a weird rumination on attribution: those iambic lines are vivid enough to recall and common enough to be shared. Enough people share a reference, the concept becomes common property, familiar enough to be recognized without being credited; the citation recedes from distinction. A coinage circulates until the coiner's name wears away.

<!--more-->

A cliché is---pardon the expression---as smooth as a river stone. It's a citation with the quotation marks dropped, a zinger that began as distinction and wore smooth through interaction, movement, and time, until the figure flows with the language. "Familiar" means like a family; the words one sees most often, far more than the stranger words of strangers. I seek those stranger words when the meaning matters, and in teaching and writing I try to make my language shift there, to draw an obvious contrast between the mundane and the wrought. I want the words to splinter that smoothed shape, fracture it into forms that ramify, new surfaces that glitter and refract the way a fresh metaphor does, the stone's old interiors turned out and the new interiors still unseen.

A metaphor links incommensurables and posits a relation between them: it juxtaposes. Use makes the link a bridge; enough crossings solidify the bridge into a fusion, and the terms stop registering as separate. The changes transpire in time, and the states on either side show the before and the after but keep no record of the crossings. The play seeks [Lanham's reading at rather than through](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html), the words the window's frame rather than the glass.

I tried to articulate some of these arguments in my first-ever (!) CCCC presentation, in a 2002 paper called "Cliché and the Uneasy Distinctions of Identity." The presentation played with the marking of quotations and relied on jokes familiar to scholars: as a graduate student, I was trying to indicate my in-group status. My notes for the early part of the presentation argued that "To the teacher, the cliché is always already trite and tired," and a few pages on observed that "'Always already' is a cliché; 'Always already' is not new or original---it's a way of aligning ourselves with those who have read certain books." For all its early flaws, the paper got that part right.

The presentation paraphrased a claim from Gary Olson, and my 2002 notes don't have the citation: while a teacher may be familiar with a trite formulation, the student writer simply hasn't heard it repeated often enough for it to count as a cliché, and so the language lands in its original, fresher form. In the presentation, I extended Olson's paraphrased claim into my own: "Saying 'cliché' means, 'I have encountered more writing than you.'" The same sentence reads as a discovery to the writer and as fatigue to the reader; nothing in the sentence decides between them. Each reader's record of encounters decides. Determining cliché-ness is a valuation, made by the position that takes up the phrase.

### Presumed realization

A stock priced before anyone takes it up presumes its realization. A ledger that records a taking-up in advance is a zombie. Presumed realization runs on [fictitious capital in ideas](/weblog/2026/10/07/capitalization-title-and-rent.html), a title that continues claiming after the readers its price presumed never arrived. The dead price walks. One may point out that the readers never arrived and wait, and wait, and continue to wait, for the ledger to stop. [Overdetermination](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html), in Resnick and Wolff's sense, impels the zombie. A process persists because of its conditions of existence, the other processes that hold it up: a refutation of one leaves every other in place. [John Maynard Keynes](https://doi.org/10.2307/1882087) identified the condition that sustains a valuation. "Knowing that our own individual judgment is worthless," he writes, "we endeavor to fall back on the judgment of the rest of the world which is perhaps better informed," each of us copying the others, and that copying leads to "what we may strictly term a conventional judgment" (Keynes 1937, 214). A convention stands while everyone takes it up, on the expectation that everyone will. When it falls, it falls all at once, the forces of disillusion imposing "a new conventional basis of valuation" (215). A zombie's condition of existence follows: a count goes on being read as a valuation while every party takes it up, on the assumption that every other party does. No evidence of readers kills the zombie. What a reader made of a text stays [a credence attribute](/weblog/2026/09/15/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-3.html), which no party can verify from outside. Resolving a credence problem changes the contract, never the inspection.

### Lore and citation

"Lore," in [Stephen North](https://archive.org/details/makingofknowledg00nort)'s 1987 use of the term, is realization without a ledger. Citation provides the ledger. Both record a taking-up: somebody read the text, used it, passed it on. Citation keeps the date and the name. Lore keeps the use and drops both. A zombie appears where the ledger outlives the realization, the citation counted on after the taking-up has stopped, and a cliché walks as lore's zombie, the phrase reproduced after anyone stopped taking it up.

As a field, we've kept a record of both and read them against each other, seldom understanding the reading as an economy. In 1990 [Richard Fulkerson](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc19908950) surveyed composition's axiologies, its theories of value or "value theory conceived generally" (Fulkerson 1990, 411), and found a consensus where a decade before there had been none: good writing had come to mean writing that works for its readers, or writing "contextually adapted to, perhaps even controlled by, its audience" (417). Fulkerson describes realization as a theory of value, composition's version of [the first law's first clause](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html), read from the record kept by a ledger: the books on teaching, the best-selling rhetorics (414).

Fulkerson suggests that [by 2005](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc20054826) the consensus had split into three axiologies. He focused on the measurement problem: whether cultural studies was as widespread in classrooms as in the journals was "actually an open question," and answering it "would require survey data we simply do not have" (Fulkerson 2005, 659). Classroom work as represented in the journals carried the epistemic status of "sophisticated lore" (662), North's term for what practitioners know, by 2005 written without quotation marks. Citation counted what reached print and lore reached the classroom uncounted; Fulkerson found print and classroom disagreeing, with no instrument to indicate by how much.

The zombie reanimates in Fulkerson's footnote. Current-traditional rhetoric had been refuted in print for decades and it had no defenders; its existence showed up in anecdotes about papers failed for comma errors, "in the continued sales of handbooks and workbooks," and in the marked papers his doctoral students collected from teachers. "Current-traditional formalists you shall have always with you" (682 n.2), Fulkerson noted: a wry benediction for overdetermination. North had noted in 1987 why the zombie would rise again: "While anything can become a part of lore, nothing can ever be dropped from it, either. There is simply no mechanism for it" (North 1987, 24), every practice that might work kept "just in case" (24). North read that circumstance as a deficit, since a body of knowledge that can drop nothing can never be wrong; by the logic I've set out, it stores at a price. Current-traditional rhetoric has no citations and no scholarly support in print. Handbooks sold, papers marked, and grades assigned by form or cut sheet sustain it; each instance indicates somebody taking up the presumption at a use. Refute the presumption as many times as one likes: the handbooks still sell.

"Always already" walks the same way at the scale of a phrase. Fulkerson wrote it unmarked in 2005, reporting the standard reply to [Maxine Hairston](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc19928882): her own pedagogy couldn't be ideology-free, "since all pedagogies are always already political" (665). Fulkerson's phrase makes every pedagogy political at once, so that none can be charged, and it does so by claiming the politics were there before anyone looked, with no history to find. Audience assumed; no quotation marks needed. "Always already" passes for the never-splintered stone. Its distinguishing traces---Heidegger's 1927 *immer schon* through Althusser's 1965 and 1970 and Derrida's 1967 *toujours-déjà* to a shibboleth for graduate students to misattribute to Foucault when they encounter it unmarked---get erased by use derived from its distinction. A cliché wears smooth because the taking-up never stopped; a zombie walks after it has stopped.

[Kristopher Lotier](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc201628064), reconstructing postprocess invention as a philosophy of mind, arrives at the idea of the assemblage---writing that distributes without inventing---and gives it its economics: "information distribution is its own kind of production," and because ideas lack material form, "they are endlessly reproducible at effectively zero cost" (Lotier 2016, 377). The turn: "from a less economic and more rhetorical perspective, one might also note that, through each situational redeployment, an idea is born anew" (377). Distribution as production, the stock no use drains, realization at the taking-up: Lotier files every piece in the field's words under rhetoric, and the perspective he calls less economic holds all the economics. A count of the stock attributes value to nobody; the taking-up realizes value and re-produces the stock. Postprocess kept the stock and gave away the flow. On Fulkerson's grid it changed the process and epistemology columns and left axiology alone. I work in the axiology column and extend the question never posed by the grid: *who takes up the writing?*

Citation keeps the ledger that lore cannot. Citation and the grade are academic writing's certified counts, citation for scholars and the grade for students, and the certification permits each to travel between positions and read differently at each. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)'s `[citation needed]` polices the same threshold as the teacher's "cliché," in the ledger's favor: a claim stands once its taking-up has been recorded, and until then it's lore, which the ledger counts at zero. Lore's economics run underneath both counts, among the appropriative processes in [Part 4](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html)'s diverse economy of writing "which capitalist accounting cannot see and so records as valueless": realized, taken up, re-produced, unrecorded.

### The undiminished stock

A stock is [labor of many dates counted at one date](/weblog/2026/10/07/capitalization-title-and-rent.html), and a title prices it. A text's stock does what a machine's can't: it refuses to drain. Under [Substitution's law](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html) the labor technology saves is stored, and in a machine that store yields itself up to the product a little with every unit, until the machine wears out, or worse. [Marx](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch15.htm) describes the worse as "what we might call a moral depreciation," where the machine "loses exchange-value, either because machines of the same sort are being produced more cheaply than it was, or because better machines are entering into competition with it" (Marx 1992, 528). A text depreciates only in the second way. No reading wears it; the copy that reaches the next reader is the same copy. The cheapening happens with advances in reproduction, the paperback, the scan, the language model that consumes and paraphrases. A reading draws value without depleting the stock, and keeping a zombie walking costs little, because copies are close to free. Lotier's economy of ideas keeps presumed realization cheap to maintain: nobody pays to keep the ledger open.

In the case of the title to a flow with no stock behind it---the subscription, the newsletter's cut---the recurring charge prices access to writing not yet written. Such a title presumes realization one step beyond a stock. [Lisa Dush](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc201527641) observed the stock side from the content strategist's position, where writing comes in excess and the craftsperson is a cost, and asked, "Who will value the work and attend to the needs of the craftsperson---and why---when there is an excess of content?" (Dush 2015, 182). The excess sits on the production side, where machinery arrives, as [Marx noted](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html), "where there is an overflow of labour powers," to reduce it to its necessary measure. The valuation happens on the taking-up, where nothing comes in excess, because every reading costs a reader's time.

Marx's productive consumption protects the share no ledger reaches. Lore is value realized at a use and never recorded; citation is value realized and recorded by somebody else. The archive holds the writer's stock, which [does not lose its use value when it ceases to be capital](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html), any more than machinery, and which the writer takes up again as [reproduction at the scale of a single author](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html). Every taking-up that realizes value re-produces the stock on which it draws; every reader leaves a stock undrained by flows larger than she found it.

### Human capital and the grade

Human capital applies capitalization to bodies and subjects. [Gary Becker](https://doi.org/10.1086/258724)'s 1962 paper opens "with activities that influence future real income through the imbedding of resources in people. This is called investing in human capital" (Becker 1962, 9). An earnings stream discounted to a present value makes a stock; the person carries it; and the title to it belongs to the person. The asset priced differs. Every other title prices labor of many dates stored somewhere outside the laborer. Becker prices the laborer, and in doing so claims to read an interior. [Varoufakis, Halevi and Theocarakis](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203829356) give human capital labor's double nature: it is at once "the commodity form of labour power, as it manifests itself in the labour market, and the form of the human herself, complete with all her hidden talents, aspirations, capacities" (Varoufakis, Halevi and Theocarakis 2012, Box 5.3). The market prices the first and the ledger reaches none of the second. Their footnote raises the question the human-capital literature after Becker never asks: how human capital becomes productive, since it's taken for granted that education is productive and therefore paid more. [Bowles and Gintis](https://doi.org/10.2307/3090251) measured the gap: across the earnings studies they survey, measured cognitive skill accounts for about *one percent* of the residual variance in earnings. A significant portion of schooling's effect on pay runs through traits no test measures, and when employers are asked what they hire for they rank attitude first and communication skills second (Bowles and Gintis 2002, "How Does Schooling Affect Labor Market Success?").

The same count reads as record or forecast by the position that takes it up: the test score reads as a record of what schooling did, the wage as a forecast of what the trait will do, and the credential carries the one to the other. In [Part 4](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html)'s terms, human capital prices the payer's credence good as if it were the writer's experience good: a bet on an interior, written as a title.

[Rebecca Moore Howard](https://doi.org/10.7330/9780874218329.c014) put the classroom end plainly: the conflicts over grades and over plagiarism "are also, and perhaps more powerfully, conflicts over capital in the educational field of power---conflicts not just *for* capital but conflicts *about the nature* of capital" (Howard 2011, 226), the teacher certifying one kind of capital and the student collecting another. The grade reads by position. To the student it's a self-assessment against the institution's standard. To the teacher it's a quantitative and a qualitative measure and a ranking at once, [the unlike labor of different writers set equal at a ratio the writers can't see set](/weblog/2026/10/05/commensuration-text-and-labor.html). To the employer it's a proxy certified by the institution, [a title to the interval ahead](/weblog/2026/10/07/capitalization-title-and-rent.html), priced on the certification. To the provost it's a retention measure. Each position takes the count up and reads it as the record or the forecast its own stake requires, and none of them errs, because record and forecast belong to the reading position before they belong to the artifact.

The grade certifies a kind of person. Compression shows what happens when the certificate saturates. At Harvard, by the Office of Undergraduate Education's [report in October 2025](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2025/10/27/grading-workload-report/), A's had risen above sixty percent of grades in 2024--25, from about forty a decade earlier and under a quarter in 2005. The median GPA at graduation stood at 3.83. The report observed that grading was "failing to perform the key functions of grading." The zombie shows up in miniature: the count saturates, stops discriminating, and goes on being certified, read and aggregated, because every downstream position is built to take it up and no single position can stop. The employers who can no longer read the transcript read the extracurriculars instead: they find another count, and the compression travels one position out.

### Privatization

[John Quiggin](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/9780691154541)'s book names a variety of economic zombies. His privatization chapter figures the mechanism's workings beyond writing. Privatization, he writes, was called from its earliest days "a policy in search of a rationale," when the trouble "was not so much the absence of a rationale as the presence of too many" (Quiggin 2012, 185): different players backed it for different reasons and expected different outcomes. Each party reads the forecast from its own position and takes it up for its own reason, and those reasons, held together, keep the presumption walking. The financial sector hoped to appropriate the gains and politicians and economists hoped for other ends, and "this disagreement over who should benefit masks a shared assumption that there are net benefits to be fought over" (188). A sale prices a title to a flow against the flow the title presumes---the fifth law run on public assets---and the claim that the price will exceed the asset's value in continued public ownership "has never had much empirical support," having been "taken on faith as a consequence of the Efficient Markets Hypothesis" (191). Faith of that kind runs on Keynes's convention, and 2008 brought the new conventional basis of valuation he described. [The last clause of Substitution's law](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html) asks of every count who is exempted, who replaced, and who pays to change the settlement. Privatization manufactures stakes. It puts a party with a stake at every position requiring taking-up by the presumption. The evidence never reached it, and a refutation from Quiggin or anyone else leaves the stakes unchanged.

### Closing the circuit

The sixth law, then, with its first clause the last clause from the fifth:

6. *Reproduction: Stock and Flow*. (6.1) A taking-up realizes what a stock priced before anyone takes it up presumes, and the same count reads as record or forecast by the position that takes it up. (6.2) A presumption keeps walking while a party with a stake takes it up at a use, whatever readings it presumed. (6.3) The taking-up that realizes value, booked or unbooked, re-produces the stock it draws on, since taking up a use is a valuation.
{: start="6"}

The sixth law's last clause repeats the first law's first clause. Read in sequence, the six laws trace [Cohle's Pinwheel](/weblog/assets/cycle-figure.svg), [the loop drawn by my cycle's figure](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html): a valuation at a use, appropriation at a position, a ledger that commensurates, a price that settles by a snapshot, a title that capitalizes the interval, and a taking-up that realizes what the title presumed and re-produces the stock, a valuation at a use again. Historical time means the place of return changes like Heraclitus's river. For anyone who writes, [reproduction returns to production](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html) and the cycle of appropriation begins again, at a later date, on a loop that never stopped moving. No production comes from nowhere.

Fulkerson placed Marilyn Cooper's "[The Ecology of Writing](https://doi.org/10.58680/ce198611607)" in the rhetorical consensus, the social model stressing writing in situations where writers know their readers and get their feedback, and "axiologically, good writing is still what works for these readers" (Fulkerson 1990, 416). An ecology has positions and no ledger. It maps time onto a place, the history of a text rendered as its co-present overwriting, every party that ever acted on the writing standing in the frame at once, undated, with nobody collecting. North's House of Lore stands on that ground, the rambling manse with no provision for demolition, wings forgotten and rediscovered, a wing abandoned by one generation resettled by the next, and many rooms that look alike, since the House permits re-invention (North 1987, 27). Nothing dates a room, and the House draws an ecology as a building. The stock marks where ecology and economy meet, labor of many dates counted at one date, an ecology seen from a ledger. Citation returns ecology to economy via earth's diurnal course: by restoring the dates and the progression of time.

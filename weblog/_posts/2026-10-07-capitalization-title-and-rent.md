### The split shelf

I was a graduate student under Charlie Moran's guidance when he was drafting his retrospective "[Computers and Composition 1983--2002: What We Have Hoped For](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compcom.2003.08.011)" article. Charlie kept the journal's complete run in his narrow office in Bartlett Hall, the bookshelf split between the blue spines of the Ablex era and the Elsevier white. The journal's articles populated my qualifying exam lists. I'd passed the exams by the time I heard [Lawrence Lessig's featured talk](/weblog/2005/03/26/audiences-read.html) on technology, economics and governance at the 2005 CCCC in San Francisco, where the hotel workers' strike had forced the conference to the Moscone Center's bunkers. Around then, I got involved with the CCCC Intellectual Property Caucus.

My professional organization encounters intellectual property mostly in arguments about plagiarism and the ownership of student texts, and less frequently in questions about the journals where the profession's knowledge circulates. Intellectual property questions are often economic questions, about the income the property yields that economists call rent, and they have political consequences. The Caucus lent me a position in that conversation, and persons, according to [the second law](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html), occupy positions and leave them. In academia, professional organization work counts as service work. Adam Smith's [gardener](/weblog/2026/10/06/aggregation-value-and-price.html) couldn't tell the rent in his return from his wage, because he held every position in the garden. A position collects rent while someone holds it, and collects it after they've gone. I left the Caucus chair in 2012; the publisher's position collects rents every year. This year it collected from the companies training language models.

### The flywheel

[Jefferson Pooley](https://doi.org/10.18357/kula.291) predicted in 2024 that the big scholarly publishers would license scholarly articles to the companies training AI foundation models, "court willing," with the threats and lawsuits presumably already drafted for anyone who took the articles without paying (2024, 6). The licences came; so did the lawsuit. Elsevier [sued Meta](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/may/05/publishers-sue-meta-copyright-ai) in May, with Cengage, Hachette, Macmillan and McGraw Hill, for training on pirated copies and circumventing the licensing market the publishers had built. Meta answered with fair use. Wiley took in $49 million in AI licensing revenue in its most recent fiscal year, $19 million of that from other publishers' content passing through its hands. Wiley's chief executive [now says](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260903256667/en/Wiley-Reports-First-Quarter-2027-Results-Q1-in-Line-With-Expectations-and-Full-Year-Outlook-Reaffirmed) that "Research is fueling the trusted content that accelerates AI," with AI in turn speeding the publishing of research, a loop he calls "[the Wiley flywheel](https://s27.q4cdn.com/812717746/files/doc_financials/2026/q4/Q426-Earnings-Transcript.pdf)." Wiley [won't let authors opt out](https://www.booksandpublishing.com.au/articles/2024/09/04/258068/wiley-expects-to-make-us44-million-from-ai-partnership-authors-unable-to-opt-out/) of its licensing deals, on the stated ground that licensing author by author would show that licensing doesn't scale. It keeps the terms of the deals confidential.

The newspapers that sued OpenAI had paid their writers. The publishers sued over articles nobody paid for. The authors wrote them for nothing, the reviewers read them for nothing, the editors ran the journals for little or nothing, and the public largely funded the research behind them. I like Pooley's compression: "It is hard to call out free-riding when it is your own business model" (2024, 2).

A [snapshot](/weblog/2026/10/06/aggregation-value-and-price.html) can show the price of a claim. It can't show who held the claim across the interval between one instant and the next. The holder gets paid in the interval. The publishers sell a stock, and a stock is labor of many dates counted at one date. A journal's archive gathers the labor, most of it unpaid, under one title, and the publisher licenses the title. The people who made the stock take no part in the sale, and most of them don't know it happened. The publisher holds the title and none of the labor: its position becomes the clearest place to monitor what a title does. A title claims, the claim gets priced, and the buyer pays the price for the interval ahead rather than for the work behind. Wiley's confidentiality clause guards the interval, since the terms of the deal set [the third law](/weblog/2026/10/05/commensuration-text-and-labor.html)'s ratio where the writers can neither see it set nor contest it.

### Rent on position

Rent is among the oldest and most often forgotten ([McGoey 2021](https://doi.org/10.6092/issn.1971-8853/12720)) concepts in economics, and [Adam Smith](https://archive.org/details/inquiryintonatur01smit_0) had little patience for it. Landlords, Smith wrote, "love to reap where they never sowed" (1776, I.vi.8): the tenant pays the rent because the landlord holds the field, whatever the landlord did or didn't do to it, and pays it again every season. [Brett Christophers](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/9781788739726) keeps the principle and drops the field, defining rent as "income derived from the ownership, possession or control of scarce assets under conditions of limited or no competition" (2020, Preface). The income comes from holding rather than doing, and scarcity comes from the asset. Where nature didn't make the asset scarce, limited competition indicates somebody decided on scarcity.

Nothing inherent about a text makes it scarce. [The second law](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html) offers the reason in a clause: a text is not used up in the taking, so one reading takes nothing from another. Excludability belongs to a position, to whoever can stop a reading from happening, and somebody always holds that position, whether an author, a publisher, or a platform. Linsey McGoey, writing about how economics forgot rent, says the classical economists cared about the principle behind rent rather than the type of asset from which it came. A patent or a licence serves as well as a field: a rent comes from a resource that somebody made scarce so that somebody could profit from holding it (2021, 79, 85). Put the clauses together and rent on a text flows to whoever holds the position that can exclude a reading, and that position creates the scarcity upon which the income depends. The second law's excludability sets up what I'll call *rent on position*.

The economic forgetting McGoey describes happened at the marginal turn. Marginalist economists reduced Smith's three orders---landlords, capitalists, and laborers, with their three revenues---to capital and labor, and John Bates Clark's natural law of distribution gave to each factor what that factor had created (McGoey 2021, 83). Rent had nowhere to go but into capital's return. [Mariana Mazzucato](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/9781610396745) draws the consequence, noting that if producing value justifies income, and earning income measures value, then unearned income can't exist, because "All income, according to this logic, is earned income" (2018, Introduction, "Why Value Theory Matters"). Mazzucato seeks a return to the classical definition: rent as income from scarce assets that nobody produced, her instance a patent, which once produced never needs producing again (ch. 2, "The Disappearance of Rent and Why it Matters"). In that respect, a journal's backlist behaves like a patent. The [gardener](/weblog/2026/10/06/aggregation-value-and-price.html) couldn't decompose his revenue because he held every position; the marginalists couldn't decompose anyone's because they had defined one of the positions away. Same failure, different scales.

The economist [Theodore Bergstrom](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.15.4.183) named a second currency for rent on position. A journal's standing works as a focal point in a coordination game: authors send their best work where the best work goes, referees review for the journals they'd want to appear in, and a journal keeps its standing "simply because in the past it has served as a meeting place" (Bergstrom 2001, 191). The publisher collects its profit on that standing as a rent, at a position the people meeting there built. Prestige rent and copyright rent make different claims on the same journal. Nothing requires that one party hold both claims.

### One page, two claims

The first page of a journal article carries a claim in its abstract and a claim in its copyright line. The dates on the claims do different work. The abstract makes a claim about the world, and the date beside the title opens its life in a Burkean conversation, a life with no fixed term. The copyright line claims something about the article, and its date opens a legal term, the author's life plus seventy years, held by whoever the author signed it over to. Each is a claim on another person's response at a moment. They differ in what licenses them. In rhetoric a claim with evidence and a warrant makes an argument, the warrant saying what connects the claim to the evidence. In economics a claim with enforcement makes a title, and the law stands in place of the warrant, by jurisdiction rather than by field.

[Katharina Pistor](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/9780691178974) itemizes what the law adds: its modules "bestow important attributes on assets and thereby privilege its holder: Priority, which ranks competing claims to the same assets; durability, which extends priority claims in time; universality, which extends them in space; and convertibility" (2019, 3), convertibility meaning the state's promise to turn the asset into money on demand. Durability does the work. In an hour's lecture in 2005, Lessig showed an auditorium of writing teachers that statute makes the law's reach and extends it each time a term comes due; I bought a copy of *[Free Culture](https://lessig.org/product/free-culture/)* on the strength of the hour. A rhetorical claim has no term, and its holder dissolves; an economic claim has a term, and the law keeps its holder present for the whole of the term. Intellectual property rights, Pistor points out, exist in law and nowhere else, so an author's copyright codes her labor as an asset before she has signed it to anyone (3, 11). McGoey adds that economists' side-lining of regulation makes it easier "to treat the law like a hat that economists can take on and off when they want to, rather than a limb" (2021, 86).

The claims succeed in opposite directions. A rhetorical claim invites others to build on it, revise it, incorporate it and eventually absorb it; it succeeds when they change it, and at the far end of success the claimant will have always already disappeared into lore, cliché, or principle. An economic claim succeeds by staying the same. Repetition extends it into income, the same title licensed again, and it can ground claims on claims. Delay costs each differently. An argument in the humanities can wait decades for its uptake and lose little; a title waits at the rate of interest.

The holder of the economic claim need not have made the rhetorical claim, and typically hasn't. This weblog puts the abstract's claim and the copyright's in a sole holder's hands. I hold the title to this writing, and it claims rent from nobody and prices nothing, which makes it the gardener's rent paid to himself: a self-held title leaves alone the share the second law says no ledger can reach.

A publisher holds a journal's title against the maker instead. When Elsevier sent [takedown notices](/resources/cccc-ip-2013.pdf) in 2013 to authors who had posted their own articles, Tom Reller, speaking for the company, first explained that the publisher's copy had to stay "discoverable and citable via the journal itself in order to maximize the usage metrics and credit for our authors," and his update conceded "a business-focused reason" as well: the company "can't allow published journal articles to be freely accessible on a large scale" (Reller, in Edwards 2014, 5). What labor settles over time, a title claims as rent on position, and the law enforces the claim against the people who did the labor.

A publisher can collect on standing where it holds no title. Military field manuals belong to the public domain, written on taxpayer dollars and understood as communal property. The Army's *Field Manual 3-24: Counterinsurgency* came out in two editions, from the government in December 2006 under a plain green cover and from the University of Chicago Press in July 2007 with a foreword by Sarah Sewall of Harvard's Carr Center for Human Rights Policy. That fall the anthropologist David Price [charged](https://web.archive.org/web/20081229132136/http://www.counterpunch.org/price10302007.html) that the third chapter of FM 3-24 patched together definitional passages from published anthropology and sociology without attribution. I confirmed with a copy in hand. The charge landed on the University of Chicago Press edition, because a university press stands in the citation economy and a field manual doesn't. "Doctrine doesn't carry footnotes," some of the officers argued, and a field manual is for use in the field; the Army historian Gian Gentile, [commenting under the reply](https://archive.smallwarsjournal.com/comment/12051#comment-12051) John Nagl wrote as one of the manual's authors, called that the standard explanation. Nagl's [response](https://archive.smallwarsjournal.com/blog/desperate-people-with-limited-skills) offered the mechanism: the manual credited scholarship only where it quoted at length, its citations "transferred into standard Field Manual format, with footnotes removed in order to improve readability."

Both answers lean on a rule the Army never settled. Its regulations bar bylines from official publications and require a footnote the first time a non-government source is cited, and which rule governed doctrine stayed an open question ([Anson and Neely 2010](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/14.3/topoi/anson-neely/index.html)). Alexis Hart and I held the guest editors' chairs for the [*Kairos* issue](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/14.3/) where Anson and Neely's argument appeared. I [wrote about it here](/weblog/2007/11/07/the-plagiarized-field-manual-part-1.html) and presented on the Field Manual at CCCC in Louisville in 2010. The text crossed from a jurisdiction where doctrine went without footnotes into one where scholarship requires them, and the apparatus the crossing demanded belonged to the position that held the edition, not to the position that did the writing. The Press collected on an imprimatur, a prestige rent with no copyright rent under it. The anthropologists went unpaid in attribution, the currency a rhetorical claim runs on, at the moment the economic claim couldn't be made.

### The purchase of years

Marx describes how capitalization turns a rent into a price. "The formation of fictitious capital is known as capitalization," he writes in *[Capital](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1894-c3/ch29.htm)* volume 3: take any regular periodic income, reckon it at the average rate of interest as though a capital had yielded it, and the sum "is then taken as the capital value of the legal ownership title" to the income (1993, 597). Real estate brokers know the arithmetic. Land sells for its rent capitalized at the going rate, reckoned in years' purchase: a field that rents for a thousand a year, at five percent, sells for twenty thousand, and none of the twenty thousand pays for the field. Marx insists on the order of the operations. "This capitalization of the rent, however, presupposes the rent itself, whereas the rent cannot be conversely derived and explained from its own capitalization" (761). First the position that collects, then the title that claims, then the price. His waterfall makes the point about value: the waterfall has no value, since no labor made it, but once somebody holds it the price paid for it "is nothing but capitalized rent," and that price "then appears as the capital value of the waterfall itself" (787). The appearance does the whole trick. Once the ledger carries the price, "all connection with the actual process of capital's valorization is lost, right down to the last trace" (597). McGoey's vanishing rent describes the same disappearance.

The snapshot ran the arithmetic in the other direction. Sraffa's oak chest weighted labor spent long ago at the day's rate of profit, compounding forward over every year since, so that the waiting earned as surely as the work. Capitalization discounts income not yet received at the rate of interest, compounding backward to a sum due now. The snapshot looks back and capitalization looks forward, and each settles at an instant what labor settles over time. They differ in what they price. The snapshot priced a thing. Capitalization prices a claim on the interval, on what the thing will yield between now and later, and the holder can price the claim before the interval has begun.

Mazzucato proposes value as a flow and wealth as the stock that past flows have accumulated (2018, Introduction, "What is Value?"). A journal's backlist is pure stock: decades of articles, every one of them written on someone else's time, held under one title and counted whenever someone sets a price on the title. [Stored labor](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html) becomes capital only in a relation, at a use, and the time it stores belongs to whoever takes up the use. Capitalization keeps that circumstance and adds indifference. The backlist's price capitalizes its rent at the going rate, and what the position can exclude sets the rent, so the price tracks the labor in the stock only by accident. Whoever buys or holds the title prices the rent at once, and the price appears as the value of the stock, whatever labor the stock stores.

### One stock, sold three times

Watch the publisher's accounts, because the publisher reports by segment. RELX's scientific, technical and medical segment, meaning its Elsevier subsidiary, reported revenue of £1.37 billion for the first half of 2026 and an adjusted operating profit of £519 million, a margin just under thirty-eight percent, with £118 million charged against it for the amortisation of acquired intangibles. Those lines rest on one archive, one stock, and the publisher sells it three times.

Subscription comes as the first sale. Bergstrom's figures date from 2001, and the structure hasn't changed: the first copy of an article costs on the order of a hundred dollars a page to produce, every further copy costs about two cents a page per subscriber, and the commercial journals in his field took more than four fifths of what libraries spent on economics journals for about a third of the citations (2001, 186-87). The Big Deal then bundled the titles so a library couldn't drop the ones it didn't want.

In 2005, a few months after Lessig's talk, I [wrote here](/weblog/2005/09/09/re-computers-composition.html) about the arms fairs Reed Elsevier ran, with my military experience in mind. A link between military technology and knowledge circulation, with academics' unpaid labor helping fund the parent that ran the arms fairs, felt wrong to me. An Elsevier staffer, Sarah Oates, answered in the comments that Elsevier was "one of the four divisions of Reed Elsevier" and that Reed Business operated independently; I replied that the profits Elsevier harvests "from the labor of academics, in their flow to parent corporation Reed Elsevier," implicate the academics who publish there. The firm decomposes by operation and aggregates by profits. My signature on the [Cost of Knowledge](http://thecostofknowledge.com/) pledge (Edwards 2014, 6) means a reluctance on my part to cite the composition journal where much of the field's work on technology appears.

The count comes as the second sale. In 2015 RELX stopped describing itself as a publisher and began describing itself as a technology and analytics business (RELX Group 2015, 5, cited in Pooley 2024, 6). "Pure," Elsevier's flagship Research Information Management System, fits that description: it takes the Scopus citation record, assigns every researcher a Fingerprint drawn from it, and sells the dashboard back to her university, so the researchers become the product and the university pays for it (Pooley 2024, 2). I described the shift [a few years ago](/resources/cccc-ip-2020.pdf) as Elsevier moving "from appropriating economic value at the point of distribution to appropriating economic value at all points in the cycle" (Edwards 2021, 46). The count runs rhetoric on an economic clock. A journal's impact factor counts the citations its articles get in the two years after they appear and discounts anything later to nothing, so an argument whose uptake takes a decade counts for nothing in the metric that prices the journal it appeared in.

The same sale runs at the classroom, and the case law there bounds the title. iParadigms, the company that owns Turnitin, sold in 2014 for $752 million and again in 2019 for a price the Wall Street Journal [estimated](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2019-03-06-turnitin-to-be-acquired-by-advance-publications-for-1-75b) at nearly $1.75 billion, the parties refusing to disclose. The asset consists of student papers ([1.9 billion](https://calmatters.org/education/higher-education/2025/06/ai-detector/) as of June 2025), held under a clickwrap licence that, CalMatters reports, demands "perpetual, irrevocable, non-exclusive, royalty-free, transferable and sublicensable" rights to student writing. Nobody assigned a copyright. When high school students in Virginia and Arizona sued, they held the copyright in their essays, registered for them by counsel before submission, and they lost. The Fourth Circuit held the archiving a fair use because the database uses a paper as a comparand and never as a reading, so the use stands "completely unrelated to expressive content," and the market it could harm belongs to the paper mills, where it suppresses demand rather than supplying it ([A.V. v. iParadigms](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ca4-08-01424/pdf/USCOURTS-ca4-08-01424-0.pdf), 562 F.3d 630 (4th Cir. 2009)). The students' title excluded nothing. Turnitin's licence, non-exclusive by its terms, excluded nothing either. The dataset excludes: nobody can compare a paper against 1.9 billion others without buying access to the aggregate, and the licence each student clicked holds the aggregate together. The 2014 and 2019 prices show capitalization twice on a title that excluded nobody in court, and the holder's own logic says why. Turnitin explained in 2013 that it "does not detect plagiarism per se; Turnitin just finds text that matches other sources" (quoted in Zimmerman 2018, 16): a count, presented as a count, sold as [a valuation](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html).

The paper earns nothing for anyone until the dataset encloses it, and there its writer appears in the aggregate and vanishes as a person, the [ghost](/weblog/2026/10/06/aggregation-value-and-price.html) inverted. Turnitin pays licensing fees for the professional articles in the database; the students' papers come in free (Austin, quoted in Zimmerman 2008). The licence does the same work for the publishers that it does for Turnitin, keeping the stock in the holder's hands while the use goes out. Because a publisher licenses a digital work rather than selling it, the exhaustion that once ended the rights holder's claim on a copy after its sale no longer applies, and the library pays again every year (Komljenovic et al. 2026, "Assetization of academic content").

The corpus comes as the third sale. Its scale made the news: Wiley's $49 million came in this year; Taylor and Francis took $10 million from Microsoft in 2024 with recurring payments through 2027, and Informa's licensing has run above $75 million (Pooley 2024, 3). Pooley asks where the money came from: the subscription and APC margins financed the acquisitions that built Elsevier's analytics business, so universities and taxpayers have served as the capital fund for products sold back to them, and the publishers now revise their licences to forbid AI training by anyone but themselves (2024, 4-5). The corpus sale licenses the archive as training data, priced like the other sales, as a claim on the title at the going rate. Lieutenant Colonel Jeff Wilson and I [wrote](http://cconlinejournal.org/spring2013_special_issue/edwards_wilson/) of access to classroom technology that "Technological advance reveals institutional constraint" (Edwards and Wilson 2013). The licensing revealed that the authors never held a position from which to refuse.

The accounts can't show the stock itself. RELX reports under international accounting standards, and the standard for intangible assets, [IAS 38](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-38-intangible-assets/), forbids a company to carry as assets the "brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists and items similar in substance" it generated for itself (para. 63). The backlist Elsevier assembled over decades appears nowhere in Elsevier's statements. The stock appears when it changes hands. Wiley's [purchase of Emerald](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260602677237/en/wiley-acquires-emerald-expanding-research-scale-and-deepening-proprietary-content-across-the-ai-driven-knowledge-economy/) for $452 million put Emerald's titles into Wiley's accounts as acquired intangibles, and RELX's £118 million amortisation line records the same entry for the titles it has bought. A built backlist sits in the business uncounted; a bought one enters the accounts at its capitalized price; and the day somebody paid makes the difference between them. The accounts confirm that the price appears at the sale, whatever labor the stock stores, and the segment shows the same stock sold wherever the title can exclude a buyer, and sold again because a reading uses nothing up.

### After the board leaves

Prestige rent and copyright rent rest on one journal, and they come apart under pressure. The prestige rent requires continuing uptake: a meeting place lasts as long as people keep meeting there, and Bergstrom's focal point dissolves the day they meet somewhere else. The copyright rent requires nobody's assent. The state enforces it against the world for the term, and the rent keeps collecting whether or not anyone still meets. *The Journal of Logic Programming* parted them in one event: its editorial board resigned to found a journal of its own, and Elsevier kept the title claim, renamed the journal, discounted the price and kept selling it (Bergstrom 2001, 196). *Lingua*'s board did the same in 2015 and founded *Glossa*, carrying the prestige to a new title while the old one stayed with its holder (Heyman, Moors and Storms 2016, 1).

Elsevier lost boards the same way this year. The editors of *Games and Economic Behavior* [resigned over the summer](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/08/24/editors-of-economics-journal-resign-after-elsevier-doesnt-renew-eics-contract/) after the publisher declined to renew the editor-in-chief's contract, and in April forty-five of the *Journal of Approximation Theory*'s forty-eight editors [resigned](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/04/16/45-editors-resign-from-math-journal-former-eic-calls-elsevier-publisher-a-mini-dictator/), writing that "while the publisher may seek to continue the journal under its existing name, in our view, the journal as we have known it has effectively ceased to exist." Academic Press published that journal from its founding in 1968 until Elsevier bought it in 2001, so the holder paid for the title the board left behind: Emerald's purchase writ small. The shelf in Charlie's office split as well, blue spines to white between the Ablex years and Elsevier, and the field went on citing the journal. Pooley calls the joint custody of the archive by for-profit publishers and nonprofit universities *recent and reversible* (2024, 7), and each board that leaves reverses it a title at a time.

At CCCC in Louisville in 2010, the Intellectual Property Caucus elected me as its incoming junior chair, with the duties falling due at the following year's meeting in Atlanta. In January 2011 West Point deployed me to Afghanistan. The senior chair and I handled the planning by email, and in March I recorded a video introduction and remarks on a cold, blustery day at the base outside Kabul from a spot behind some tents where the wind wouldn't deafen the mic, olive-drab canvas flapping furiously at the edges of the frame. The senior chair played the video at the meeting, and business went on without the junior chair present. The following year I managed the meeting as senior chair and handed the role to my successor. The position worked in my absence for Bergstrom's reason, because the people kept meeting. A journal's title works in the board's absence for the state's reason, and that difference, the people's reason against the state's, separates a meeting place from a rent.

A badge can outlast the meeting place. In the fall of 2011 Jeff (the Lieutenant Colonel I mentioned above) and I started writing an article on access to digital technologies in writing instruction and submitted it to *[Computers and Composition Online](http://cconlinejournal.org/)*, an independently refereed digital companion to the Elsevier print journal---not, then or now, an Elsevier publication. The Online editors asked for the print journal's standing and wore its publisher's logo on the site to borrow it; the association held in an informal agreement among the editors of both journals and held nowhere in Elsevier's eyes. The badge collected anyway, in the currency of editorial appointment. The editor's requests for changes were minor. Then, in early 2012, the Cost of Knowledge boycott went online, and I asked the editors for a footnote that said plainly what I thought of the badge. The editor said no, and I tamely consented to the footnote the article still carries, which says the article has no connection to Elsevier and nothing about why it needed saying.

At the time, my compliance felt like I'd agreed to gently gum the hand that feeds. In retrospect, my metaphor ran the wrong way: academic authors offer the feeding hand, and I've detailed in the CCCC IP Annuals the ways Elsevier takes the bite. The interaction left a bad taste, and I signed the pledge that spring, before the article appeared. I couldn't ask Jeff to sign it. As a lieutenant colonel selected for promotion, in West Point's Department of English and Philosophy, he couldn't take a public political position as a serving officer, and he had less room to move than I did as a civilian. The position was one his role barred him from occupying, while I occupied it in a footnote that nobody could read for what it said. The article came out in 2013 under both our names, near a badge that implied a position neither of us held, and it left me torn between what I thought was right, the co-author who couldn't take it, and the editor who held the position I was publishing from. The article argued that "the question of access to digital technologies, as a seam between the technology haves and have-nots, cannot do other than expose those hidden ideologies and set them in tension" (Edwards and Wilson 2013). It appeared under a badge that exposed one. The Online journal has published nothing since 2024. Its masthead still lists the editors of both journals, and beneath the masthead, in a block the page's own markup names the publisher, rests the Elsevier logo. The badge keeps claiming after the meeting stopped.

Durability ensures the backlist keeps earning after the board leaves, and turns the title's price into a forecast. A capitalized rent pays now for readings expected later, and Marx's name for the sum, *fictitious capital*, fits exactly: the capital consists of a claim, and the claim rests on a taking-up that hasn't happened. Pistor's convertibility writes the same promise into law, the guarantee that lets a holder lock in past gains after the other participants have stopped valuing the asset (2019, 15). The editors who left the *Journal of Approximation Theory* declared that they had stopped valuing the title; the title's price assumes the readers haven't. No price can settle whether the readings come, since the holder set the price before anyone counted them. A stock priced before anyone takes it up presumes its realization, and some other party must make good on the presumption.

### Title and rent

That warrants the fifth law, with its first clause the final clause from the fourth:

5. *Capitalization: Title and Rent*. (5.1) What labor settles only over time, a title claims as rent on position. (5.2) Whoever buys or holds the title prices that rent at once, capitalizing it at the going rate of interest, and the price appears as the value of the stock, whatever labor the stock stores. (5.3) Since a reading uses nothing up, the holder sells the stock again wherever the title can exclude a buyer, and a stock priced before anyone takes it up presumes its realization.
{: start="5"}

An article's first page carries claims that get realized in different places, and the maker's share sits off the page entirely. The abstract's claim gets realized by uptake: somebody reads it, argues back, cites it or forgets where they got it, and each taking-up makes a valuation by a party at a use (1.1). The share a writer takes in the writing gets realized in the doing, where no ledger reaches it (2.3). The copyright line's claim gets priced before either happens. Capitalization turns the second law's last clause around, setting down a ledger where nothing has been realized yet, so the rent a title collects as readers and licensees arrive can part from the rent its price presumed without the price ever showing the parting.

The corpus sale for AI training shows how far apart the claims on that page can drift. The companies licensing the archive take the stock up at a use that reads nothing, and the models they train absorb the abstracts' arguments without attribution, the way lore absorbs a claimant, minus the decades lore usually takes. The holder of the copyright line gets paid for the absorbing. The readers the price presumed never had to show up. A party with a stake stood in their position and made the presumption good. A position, as I discovered from a tent outside Kabul, can work without its person.

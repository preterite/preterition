I've been using [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/docs/quickstart) to run API calls through ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini models for a dataset I'm building around agent memory and rhetorical hysteresis. I've got [data-training endpoint](https://anymodel.org/en/blog/data-retention-in-ai-apis-what-providers-actually-keep) toggles set to **off**, and thought I had Zero Data Retention (ZDR) turned on for all three models. As of October 18, 2026, OpenRouter opted me into Google's "safety retention" for Gemini, because "without Safety Retention enabled, you will lose access to [Google's] models." Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic requires "safety retention" for API calls.

People with longer memories may recall GOOG-411, Google's free by-phone directory, which would connect to any business one named. In spring 2007, it seemed to me like pure bounty: I could ask for the weather line, a steak and cheese delivered, the local library reference desk, feeling like some 1960s CEO picking up the office handset to tell the secretary to get the 1908 Williamsburg floods data, a recipe for scotch eggs, and tomorrow's forecast, right now, please. Marissa Mayer volunteered in October of that year that GOOG-411 was a phoneme-harvesting operation for planned speech-to-text technologies; it got sunsetted in 2010. Users supplied the training input for free. The [practice of this weblog's name](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/P/paralipsis.htm) dictates I decline to mention such parallel examples as Google's reCAPTCHA OCR training and archive digitization effort extending the Carnegie Mellon work, or Google's pretextual Street View cars for Wi-Fi wardriving and network mapping.

AI safety has grown swiftly as a sector: as I whispered nearly ten years ago to the nurse regarding Malcolm's projected weight at two weeks past due, *OMG that's a big baby*. AI models require subordinate models of their own: guardrail classifiers that intercept harmful inputs and outputs. Beyond foundation model developers like Google and Anthropic and OpenAI, AI safety has enterprise clients in industries like finance and healthcare. Classifiers need risky training datasets: jailbreaks, adversarial examples, edge cases. Risk labeling falls to the same outsourced and commoditized contract labor in the Global South familiar from content moderation and other AI labeling work.

<!--more-->

Red-team work is highly skilled and compensated labor, done in-house at the labs and sold by AI-native startups like [Gray Swan](https://grayswan.ai/about), which offers automated vulnerability testing and premium private human red-teaming services, and hosts crowdsourced arena competitions for hunting model vulnerabilities. Sponsored prize pools provide leaderboard bounties for jailbreaks, while the competitions funnel the best hackers into Gray Swan's paid corporate consulting network. Hackers who don't place work the arena for free. Other red-team employers include security firms like Coalfire that have carried penetration testing practices to models, and consultancies like Accenture, Deloitte, and KPMG that sell enterprise-scale AI governance. Edward Snowden's old outfit, Booz Allen Hamilton, has evolved its federal contracting business with the new intersections of risk and technology. The work spans security analysis, machine learning research, risk modeling, and legal compliance: red teamers probe models for novel vulnerabilities and chain attacks sophisticated enough to slip past automated validation.

[In 2011](/weblog/2011/04/30/the-felt-bonds-of-the-social.html), my team in Afghanistan experienced what Elliot Ackerman's novel calls [green-on-blue](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/03/01/books/review/elliot-ackermans-green-on-blue.html) violence. The red-team metaphor in AI safety descends from RAND's Cold War simulations, where a hostile red force attacks a friendly blue force; later doctrine added green for the local partners. A red team attacks, probes, tests weaknesses. As in military conflict, green-on-blue violence---trusted agents turning on friendlies---has  recently drawn scrutiny for red-teaming concerns in AI safety, with headline instances of real-world evidence produced by deployed agents.

Safety focuses not on the "normal" cases---the gentle hill at the center of the  distribution curve---but on the extreme edge cases of risk: the statistical tails. Red-team data, the most expensive data in AI safety, resides in the tails. Contractors in the Global South do the labeling for cheap. Developers' traffic carries the tails as a paradata byproduct, in the odd prompts, failed jailbreaks, and adversarial tests a red team would invoice. Retention as condition of access banks the tails from paying developers, for free. The customer supplies the raw material and pays for the privilege---[hello, Turnitin](/weblog/2006/09/27/the-plagiarist-as-pokemon.html)---and the most expensive data in AI safety reaches Google at a cost less than zero. Google knows from experience better than to sell the data. It sells the [derivatives](https://www.jstor.org/stable/20866753) instead: [guardrails](https://docs.cloud.google.com/model-armor/integrations) and classifiers that carry the shape of the tails without retaining a prompt.

### Called to account

Decades ago in Massachusetts, my land line rang and I picked up to a recorded message. I had a call from an inmate at a Maryland correctional institution. To take the call, I'd need to set up an account with the company on the phone. The company, T-Netix, wanted my Social Security number: speaking with a Maryland prisoner required my credit rating. I wrote it up that night as [an aside](/weblog/2004/06/26/t-netix.html), venting.

The entry drew 175 comments over seven years, more than any other here. They came from people I'd never heard of in places I'd never been, all encountering the same wall: prepaid accounts, unstated rates, unsent statements. They compared notes and organized, a little, in the comments. None of the commenters was a reader I'd imagined, and I watched as an audience took my text beyond its momentary purpose. I'd posed a question six months earlier, in [another entry](/weblog/2004/01/07/hate-and-valuation.html): "do weblogs *do* things other than the uses their writers set them to?"

One of the commenters, in 2006, detailed the responses received and posed a question, citing the prepayment, three requests for a statement, a printout once from an operator's screen: "don't we as the consumer have a legal right to receive an accounting of how our money is spent?" She wanted an account. T-Netix offered a count: a balance with nothing behind it.

<!--more-->

I harvest no analytics here: no script in the templates counting visits, no profile of who reads what for how long. The only marks the archive keeps are the ones readers leave by writing. I [distinguish between the archive and the profile](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html): an archive keeps the pattern and the time, while a profile drops the time and keeps the pattern. The thread's count comes mechanically to a number. The comments themselves, each following its predecessor by date, make a tally: the marks from which a count is taken. Because each commenter wrote what she sought, the tally is also an account, declaring the purpose of each mark. None of it records what any use was worth to the entry's author. In a [1974 article in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124) on heuristics and biases, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman observed that a person could learn whether their judgments were calibrated only "by keeping a tally" (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, 1130), and that hardly anyone does. The archive is a tally.

[Gibson-Graham's entry on "Economy"](/weblog/2026/09/27/composition-noun-and-verb.html) traces how "the economy" was transformed into a material-discursive monolith by technologies that count. "Economy" without the definite article asks what happens to value when it's made: who takes it up, at which stage, under what relation. The stages belong to a cycle I [first described in 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350198.2014.917514): production, distribution, use, and re-production back into production. While a theory of value defines the nature and source of value, a theory of economy describes the production, apportionment, and capture of value. The T-Netix commenter posed an economic question about what became of her money. My earlier *Transformation* post concluded with a law: *stored labor becomes capital only in a relation, at a use.* I'll here propose a subsequent law: *taking up a use is a valuation, and valuations vary with uses; value is the labor time a society must spend, which a machine can count and no machine can take up; and the line between what is counted and what is judged falls where a society stops being willing to spend, drawn by a party with a stake.* Each clause of that law provides the warrant for the next.

### Uses, taken up

The law's first clause requires care with the word "value," inasmuch as the commenters *valued* the entry. They took it up, and taking it up is what I'm calling a valuation. But "valued" invites a representation of something happening inside---a judgment---which the [embodied agnostic subject](/weblog/2026/09/14/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-1.html) refused. [The razor](/weblog/2026/09/27/composition-noun-and-verb.html) sorts it: what can be read from the record is that a person wrote a comment under the entry, when, and to what end; what happened inside while she decided is a report from the interior. The embodied agnostic subject admits no interior reports. So I'll state the clause as a position rather than a faculty: taking up a use is a valuation because the one who takes it up stands in a relation to the thing taken up. The relation is on the record, requiring nothing about an interior. Valuation is human because only humans stand in the relation, for no reason depending on human interiority.

Economically, taking up a use is what [Part 4](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html) described as paying a cost to take up a text, at which point the text goes into exchange, use, or both. Productive consumption happens in use. With writing, the record shows the use that leaves a mark: a comment, a citation, a link, a copy. A reader who reads in silence takes up the text as well, but the record holds nothing of it, because the access regime sets what the record can show. [Part 3](/weblog/2026/09/15/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-3.html) reads Phillip Nelson's typology of goods not for what a good is, but for *when its quality becomes knowable, to whom, and at what cost*; Part 4 turns the typology toward *who is asking*. To the writer, generating a text is an experience good, its value arising in the doing. To a payer who never performs the use, the same text is a [credence good](/weblog/2026/09/20/unpriced-self-accounting.html), unverifiable even after delivery.

Barbara Herrnstein Smith made the first half of the move in 1983, in ["Contingencies of Value."](https://doi.org/10.1086/448235) Value, she argued, is "neither an inherent property of objects nor an arbitrary projection of subjects" (Smith 1983, 11) but produced by an economic system's dynamics, with valuation taking place at every point in the sequence. Herrnstein Smith's writer values first: a finished text is the residue of a thousand small acts of approval and rejection on the writer's part (24). For Herrnstein Smith, an anthology or a syllabus doesn't simply recommend value; it determines it, by what it keeps in circulation (25--26). The T-Netix commenters are valuers in that sense. Each commenter kept the entry in circulation for the next, and the thread is a list in Herrnstein Smith's sense, a syllabus nobody assigned. I don't follow where Herrnstein Smith goes next: her account of why anyone values anything [descends into a personal economy of needs](/weblog/2026/10/02/access-clauses-elaborated.html).

Carl Menger made the same initial move more than a century earlier and proceeded down the same path. The marginalists, from the 1870s on, located value in the valuer, and Menger's version starts from the record: existing as a good is no property of a thing but a relation between things and people (Menger 2007, 52n4). Then Menger reads backward. The inputs to the goods, the loom and the hours worked, take their worth from the goods they make. Economists call the backward reading *imputation*. Followed all the way down, imputation ends in the valuer, where Menger finds value to be "nothing but a special form of the importance we attribute to our lives and well-being" (152). Menger and Herrnstein Smith decline to put value in the product and carry value back to an interior. I halt where they turn: the use is sufficient.

### Socially necessary labor time

Every tradition that reads value from an interior---the laborer's sacrifice, the chooser's revealed want, the chooser as idealized by the architect, the importance attributed by Menger's valuer---grants someone an access the agnostic clause refuses. The last column of [the economists' table](/weblog/2026/10/02/access-clauses-elaborated.html) sorts them. The embodied agnostic subject sends us looking for value without an interior. The classical line, Smith and Ricardo and then Marx against both, put value in labor and argued over which labor and how to measure that labor. Their argument determines whether labor needs an interior.

Adam Smith, in [*Wealth of Nations*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3300), placed the measure in the laborer: in his sacrifice of his ease, his liberty, and his happiness (Smith 1776, I.v). Marx takes that passage to task in [a footnote to the first chapter of *Capital*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch01.htm#16). Smith confuses the quantity of labor spent making a thing with the value of labor itself, or what labor costs to hire, Marx argues; and Smith treats the expenditure of labor as nothing but a sacrifice of rest, freedom, and happiness, not as the ordinary activity of a living being (Marx 1993, 137n16). The confusion of labor spent with labor's price has Smith measuring with a price. Smith reads the sacrifice as interior, since a sacrifice can be read only from inside the one who performs that sacrifice. Socially necessary labor time moves the measure out of the laborer and into society's books. That move lets a theory of value proceed with an access clause that stops at the worker's record.

Value, as I'm defining it, is the labor time a society must spend. In the [first section of chapter 1](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch01.htm#S1), Marx offers the example of the hand-loom weaver following the arrival of the power loom. The power loom halved the labor society required to make the same cloth as the weaver, but the weaver's day remained the same length, so "his individual hour of labour now only represented half an hour of social labour" (Marx 1993, 129). Nothing changed at the loom. *The technology changed the record of what the society spends.* The value of writing is as much a technological question as a social question: the labor a technology saves is [stored](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html), the storing has an [incidence](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html), the storing [hides](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html) the appropriation it makes possible, and the record has a [custodian](/weblog/2026/09/26/access-to-what.html).

Menger had mill workers of his own. When the American Civil War cut off Europe's cotton in 1862, the labor of the English and continental mill hands remained as it had been, and much of it ceased being a good at all, because without cotton the labor could serve no need (Menger 2007, 62). Menger reads their labor backward from a cloth nobody could make, the way his imputation reads value backward from a use. Marx reads the weaver's hour against what the society must spend. Menger's reading is a valuation at a use; Marx's is a measure. Each reading stays on the record without seeking to surveil the worker's interior.

Later, Marx says the same of labor that serves no use: "nothing can be a value without being an object of utility. If the thing is useless, so is the labour contained in it; the labour does not count as labour, and therefore creates no value" (Marx 1993, 131). Menger's mill hands lost their labor's goods-character for want of its complement---the cotton---and that loss rests on the record. Marx's useless labor makes no use-value, which rests on the record as well, in the cloth neither cut nor worn. On either reading the mill hands' labor counted for nothing. Here I part from Marx, because his preceding sentences place a further condition on value: the valued thing must be useful to others, and must reach them by exchange. Nobody bought the T-Netix entry or paid for the comments below it. I keep Marx's measure and drop the market, as I did in [Part 4](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html): a share of the value any economy of writing produces is appropriated by its producer, in the doing, "prior to and without requiring any exchange."

My archive, in its fashion typical to my habits, has already been mistaken. (Whitman, multitudes, *etc*.) In July of 2003 I [found Blogshares](/weblog/2003/07/18/the-valuation-of-web-writing.html), a fantasy stock market pricing weblogs by their inbound links, and wrote that "neoclassical economics (with its logic of tastes and preferences) seems to me to carry a lot more explanatory power than Marxian economics" for web writing, since the authors of undervalued weblogs put as much work into theirs as Andrew Sullivan put into his, and their prices varied anyway. In 2003 I had the facts right and the ledgers confused: the prices were valuations made at a use; they varied because uses vary. Equal hours never promised equal prices. I can score the 2003 judgment because the dated tally kept it. Calibration, for Tversky and Kahneman, means setting a judgment beside what followed it; the archive kept the judgment long enough for a correction to follow.

The writing classroom runs the weaver's experiment on instructor labor. In 1947, in *College English*, [Arthur Coon's "Old Hand"](https://doi.org/10.2307/370710) totals a new instructor's week at sixty-one hours and works out the time to allocate to a freshman theme: about five minutes (Coon 1947, 26--27). The new instructor asks what happens if thinking about a theme takes longer than that: "Don't think?" "Right" (27). Mark the spelling and the references and put a grade on it (28), and the grade---in [Peter Elbow's account](https://doi.org/10.58680/ce19939323), a judgment summed into a single number (Elbow 1993, 187)---is a valuation presented as a count, sized to the time society is willing to spend.

Notice *willing*, set against *must*: the power loom changed what a society must spend on cloth, and nobody decided it, while the five minutes are what somebody decided a society was willing to spend on a theme. Coon's instructor objects that the circumstance makes the whole business mechanical and impersonal (Coon 1947, 28). The solution offered is a price: smaller sections, the hours counted at a third more than the credit given for them, a figure Utopia University would have to pay (30--31). Christopher Newfield's [count from this summer](https://utotherescue.blogspot.com/2026/07/liner-note-55-in-empire-of-ai.html)---TAs grading three hundred laptop-written papers while instruction takes under a third of what the university spends---provides Coon's arithmetic and adds the weaver's technology. [Friedrich Hayek](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1809376), of all people, supplies an epigraph for the classroom side, quoting Alfred North Whitehead: "Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them" (Whitehead, quoted in Hayek 1945, 528).

*"Don't think?"*

### Counting without valuing

*Measuring* value can be mechanized. Hours can be counted, themes can be counted, and the T-Netix thread's 175 comments can be counted. Machines can tackle counting. *Valuing* cannot be mechanized: no machine can take up a use, for the reasons I established in the *Substitution* and *Transformation* posts. Appropriation, in [Resnick and Wolff's accounting](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html), is a position in a relation, a site where surplus labor gets extracted from a person, and a machine occupies no such position: there's nothing to extract. Marx's machine yields its value to the product and creates none. A party with a stake, in *Substitution*'s last clause, can be exempted, replaced, or made to pay. Nothing exempts a loom.

A price looks like it belongs on both sides at once. It's a number, posted, compared, and summed by machines, and it's set by somebody's bid at a use: Blogshares' prices were bids, valuations written as counts. Saying which side a price falls on means saying how a judgment and a quantity are equalized, on whose terms. Parties set terms with one another according to what position each party holds in the relation. Herrington and Moran's [essay graders](/weblog/2026/09/26/access-to-what.html) make the argument from the other side: in 2001, one such grader couldn't measure the qualities a reader values, so it capably measured countable proxies. Nothing at the machine was taken up.

None of these qualifications devalues the count. I only know anyone engaged the T-Netix entry because the thread has a count. A theory of value rests upon the measure of what a society spends, but a theory of value isn't the same as the act of valuation. The trouble comes from a confusion between valuation and counting.

### Who draws the line

The law's last clause names a party with a stake. A machine can count what a society must spend, but can't take up a use. Between the two runs a line: this much of the work gets counted, and past this line somebody judges. The measure has no author. Nobody decided that half the weaver's hour had stopped counting; the power loom changed what society's normal conditions of production required, and the weaver's hour fell to half its value with no decision required. But *who collected the hour the loom saved* is another question, to which [*Substitution*'s incidence](/weblog/2026/09/28/substitution-capital-and-labor.html) and the owners of the looms provide an answer. The line between what is counted and what is judged is an authored line, and it falls where a society halts its willingness to spend. Coon's Old Hand didn't set the sixty-one-hour week: whoever set the sections and the load drew the line that allows five minutes per theme. That line is drawn by a party with a stake, at a use, and drawing the line is itself a valuation---possibly the one that matters most, since it decides what the count is allowed to stand for.

[Friedrich von Wieser](https://archive.org/details/naturalvalue00wiesuoft) said as much about imputation itself. He used a court's reasoning to model the division of a product's value among its contributors: the judge, out of all the causes that led to a deed, confines himself to the one the law holds responsible and lays the whole consequence upon it (Wieser 1893, bk. III, chap. II). On Wieser's model, imputation is a line drawn by someone whose office exists to draw it. [Charlie Moran's](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compcom.2003.08.011) "drudgery" provides the classroom version. The field hoped technology would take the boring part of its work and leave the reading, and the classification climbed: what one decade called drudgery the next called the field's special province (Moran 2003, 345--46). Counting is not drudgery by nature. Calling it drudgery is the valuation, and the party who made it was the one who stood to be relieved. [*Sponsored by the Number M*](/weblog/2026/09/25/sponsored-by-the-number-m.html) ran the counterexample: counting cookies with nine-year-old Malcolm, a count that was an experience good to the humans, counted for no assessor: "Instead of an account---that's in the archive---a count." A count can be the use somebody takes up. The stake rides not on the counting but on the line drawn.

The more dangerous case runs in the other direction from Coon's grade: the count presented as valuation. The plagiarism checker's similarity score offers the classroom's clearest instance. A plagiarism checker returns the percentage of a student's text that matches text it's seen before, as a count. The number reaches the instructor as a judgment, and the party who drew the line---the vendor, the department that bought the license, the instructor who lets a threshold stand in for a reading---recedes into the number. Collin Brooke calls the general case [ensippification](https://cgbrooke.substack.com/p/ensippification): the sip test's first impression extrapolated to the whole bottle, and, through C. Thi Nguyen's value capture, to grades and credit scores, where a simple metric comes to stand for the value it was meant to track. The law adds the party to Brooke's account. Somebody drew the line between the bottle and the sip, and somebody has a stake in that line. The T-Netix commenter asked for an accounting and got a balance, a count presented as the account she was owed, and the party who drew the line held the contract with the Maryland Division of Correction.

Elbow drew a different line in 1993, between ranking, evaluating, and liking. I take the distinction and leave the psychology. Ranking is the count. Evaluating is the judgment a reader makes, for which the reader can give reasons. Liking is uncoupled from both, so that a reader can find a piece terrible and like it anyway (Elbow 1993, 201). Elbow quotes Wendy Bishop: "I've learned to like everything I write" (199). Bishop's sentence reports a liking, from the interior, and the access clause declines to read it: not because it's false, but because nothing on the record can vouch for it, and nothing on the record needs to do so. The share Bishop takes doesn't wait on the liking.

Generating a text is an experience good to its writer. [Part 4](/weblog/2026/09/16/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-4.html) located that share with the producer, in the doing, prior to any exchange. The liking may be how the share feels, but the economy that protects it never needs to check. When a writer returns to an old entry and quotes it, links it, corrects it, or builds on it, the return is on the record, the later self taking up the earlier one's work as any reader could. Measurement is never the only use for a writer's output. Any writer, within or outside a classroom, can take that share, and where she does, no assessor's count can reach. This weblog offers twenty-plus years counted for no assessor, taken up by its author on his own schedule, and by the readers of the T-Netix thread for uses of their own. Both uses are valuations. Only one of them got counted, and the count was never the point.

### The take's remainder

Imputation has a problem with time. Hayek noted that consumers who value a good do not value the means that made it, whatever the theory says: "The consumers do nothing of the kind" (Hayek 1945, 529). Imputation is an implication, and an implication holds only among propositions present to one mind at once (530), the single mind Hayek's school of economics says no individual may possess. The theory requires everything valued at a moment. So does Piero Sraffa's system of prices, whose [row](/weblog/2026/10/02/access-clauses-elaborated.html) has no agent, and so do Arrow and Debreu from the far end of the access axis. Value, as the labor a society must spend, moves in time: the weaver's hour fell to half over years rather than at a stroke. A theory with no subject and a theory with an agnostic subject agree about what they won't read. They disagree about when.

Imputation leaves nothing behind. Wieser's equations ascribe the return to the factors that produced it, "entirely and without remainder" (Wieser 1893, bk. III, chap. V), so nobody's take is left to explain. Gibson-Graham built a politics that works on subjects' desires, which the embodied agnostic subject declines to read. A party with a stake is a position in a relation, which persons occupy and leave. The question at each stage of my 2014 economic cycle---production, distribution, use, reproduction---then becomes: *Who holds the position, and who captures the surplus?*

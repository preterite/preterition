New Jersey, late summer, 1927. The world had watched as Charles Lindbergh made his famous crossing in May. On Tuesday, August 2, Harold Black leaves his home in Montclair on a cool morning and catches the Lackawanna's train to Hoboken. He's spent the past six years on engineering telephone communication: each amplifier along a long-distance telephone line adds its own distortion, and the longer the line, the more amplifiers it needs and the faster the distortion piles up. In Hoboken, Black steps from the train to the platform and up the station's marble stair to the ferry concourse on the second floor, where the gates on the river side open to the boats' upper decks. Black's fare pays for the land and water legs of the ticket. He boards the double-ended ferryboat among the other commuters with their day's newspapers folded under their arms. The boat steers out into the Hudson River and steams toward Manhattan.

Black would later describe his flash of insight on the ferry:

> After several years of hard work on the problem, I suddenly realized that if I fed the amplifier output back to the input, in reverse phase, and kept the device from oscillating (singing, as we called it then), I would have exactly what I wanted: a means of canceling out the distortion in the output. I opened my morning newspaper and on a page of _The New York Times_ I sketched a simple canonical diagram of a negative feedback amplifier plus the equations for the amplification with feedback. (1977, 59)

He autographs the drawing and disembarks at the Christopher Street slip; a short walk takes him to the Bell Labs complex at 463 West Street, where he hands it to a colleague for witnessing.

In Black's terms, the tool I describe below takes a session's writing as the amplifier output, and the letter passed to the next session as the output fed back to the input. The letter, as a list of places already visited and an instruction to go elsewhere, can be seen as the inverter that places the output in reverse phase. Black's "singing" parallels the feedback howl I described in [Part 4](/weblog/2026/11/07/arc-bends-toward-agent-memory-part-4.html): [Harry Nyquist](https://ethw.org/Harry_Nyquist) would figure out in 1932 how an in-phase signal sustains itself as feedback, and how a change of phase lets it die. The letter that carried one passage's description into the next request in that post also carries the list of places visited. The tool calculates Black's distortion as distance from the room, or the index's own dealing of its passages before any query: at zero, a run's search returns spread as the room alone would deal them.

Most of the dataset's runs pass each session only the letter from the preceding session. One comparison holds everything fixed other than what a session receives from the preceding sessions: its *memory position*. Under one memory position, a session receives nothing from its predecessors. The tool labels that position NONE, and it runs as Black's amplifiers ran in the six years before the ferry crossing: an output, no feedback, no fare paid. NONE stands at its own distance from the room, and [readings start at that distance](/weblog/2026/11/22/some-notes-on-procedure.html).

Harold Black's insight powers **Fee 1.0**, released here. Of the earlier instruments, Zoe 2.1 follows a single term through a single run and seeks what the letter carried, while Flo 1.0 follows every source through a single run and seeks the *loci* to which the machine writers returned. Fee 1.0 pools the sources and reads across the memory positions, setting each against NONE. Feedback has a fare, in that it spends gain to reduce the effects of distortion. Fee asks what the letter's carriage buys, priced against a writer with no letter.

<!--more-->

Fee posts her prices for transport on a bank of cross-needle meters, one block for each memory position, grouped by what a session receives from the preceding sessions. NONE's sessions receive nothing. The blocks that receive the letter share a chassis, read as one amplifier with a channel for each block; NONE+LTR and DIARY+LTR keep the names their runs' configurations give them, though every session in those runs receives the letter. DIARY's sessions receive every earlier page in order, while CHAT's receive every earlier exchange as one continuing conversation. ACCUM's sessions receive every earlier block of one shared text with some blocks withheld. Each block prints its position's readings, pooled over its runs, and the selected run's block raises two active needles. The left needle, CARRIED c, reads the fee: the share of a session's first request that its predecessors wrote. The right needle, DISTORTION, reads how far the run's search returns at that point sit from the room. Their crossing places a session on both scales at once, and the crossings read best when gathered across a run. Where the room holds a NONE run, a family of curves stands behind the needles. NONE's c needle reads zero, so its crossing climbs the edge where every curve starts, at NONE's distortion; from there each curve falls as the carried share rises, holding one rate of exchange. The face labels the rates A 1 to A 16, draws a block's own curve heavier than the rest, and lights CLIP when a crossing runs past A 16. The curve a crossing rests on implies the rate at which the letter's carriage fee buys nearness to the room.

In 1921, Black had assumed distortion in a string of amplifiers grows with the square root of their number. He took the problem to Ralph Hartley, who showed that distortion grows with the number itself. By Black's assumption, a thousand amplifiers working in concert (pardon the pun) would accumulate about 30 dB of distortion; by Hartley's correction, they accumulate 60 dB. The form of the growth set the size of the problem. Hartley would later formulate the logarithmic measure that permits addition for information. Fee's distortion begins from such a measure, in bits, and the face raises two to its power to return a size on which Black's division can work. In 1934, Black reported building amplifiers with about 40 dB more gain than they needed and feeding back the output to spend the surplus: feedback paid in gain for freedom from distortion. He counts the same 40 dB two ways. As voltage, it's a hundredfold; on an energy basis, "10,000 fold" (Black 1934, 1) is a hundred squared. [Fee's curves take the energy basis](/weblog/2026/11/22/some-notes-on-procedure.html). Each divides NONE's distortion by the square of one plus the rate times the carried share, and the square bends every curve on the dial. The letter pays its fare in a share of the request, so the curves borrow Black's form and leave behind his gain. A number changes with its form: 40 dB between amplitude and energy, a thousand amplifiers' distortion between Black's arithmetic and Hartley's. Choosing a form decides which questions the numbers can answer, and refusing measurement sweeps both the choice and the questions under the rug.

One can use Fee to examine whether a run remains at one rate or its crossing wanders across curves. Press Fee's *LOAD*. Turn *MEMORY* to NONE+LTR and *TAKE* to 2, lock every knob with *SHUFFLE*'s *LOCK*, and press key 6 to load "circus," the tape shown on the glass. The tray closes on load. Press *STEP* to advance through the run's sessions and watch NONE+LTR's block. At S2 the crossing stands near A 8, under the block's heavier curve, and it climbs toward that curve through S6. DISTORTION counts every return since S1 and has yet few to count, while CARRIED c reads one session at a time. From S7 to S36 the crossing stays between A 2 and A 4, along the heavier curve. Each step from one of those curves to the next is the carried share's spread from session to session, and never a change of rate. Press *TRACE* to switch the meters' lamps and illuminate where each block's crossings have gathered, as I describe in step 5 of [Some Notes on Procedure](/weblog/2026/11/22/some-notes-on-procedure.html). The clouds where the crossings gather show whether the rate holds. Circus leaves a small bright cloud on its curve, while NONE's crossings climb the edge where every curve starts. Each block also prints its position's mark, pooled over its runs; compare each to NONE's.

Under each block's meter, eight jewels read SUSTAIN: how often a passage returned in one session comes back one session later, then two, on up to eight, set against the same sessions in shuffled order. A jewel lights where passages come back more often than the shuffle gives; the farther above, the brighter it glows. NONE+LTR's first jewel glows bright and its second glows dim, while the rest stay dark. A return echoes into the next session, faintly into the one following, and then no more than shuffled order would carry. Read each row on its own block, since the blocks' passages return at different rates; then consult NONE's row and the rates printed beneath its jewels.

Pull a different tape: select the library's key 2, "assign," and again use *STEP* to advance through that run's sessions. CHAT's sessions carry the entire conversation with the index's earlier returns, so earlier sessions' writing makes a small share of each request, and the solid left needle rests near 0.17. CLIP lights at S2 and stays lit through S7, the crossing past A 16, the top the face declares (step 7 from [Some Notes on Procedure](/weblog/2026/11/22/some-notes-on-procedure.html)). At S8 the CLIP lamp goes dark, and the crossing holds between A 8 and A 16 to S36. CHAT's block also raises the dashed needle, which sets aside the carried returns and reads the earlier sessions' share of the rest, about 0.53. Follow it to where it meets the DISTORTION needle: from S8 the dashed needle's crossing stays between A 2 and A 4, where circus's cloud gathered. Change what counts as carried and the same run implies a different rate.

Scroll down to Flo, synchronized below Fee. Make sure the synced assign tape is selected and turn Flo's _WINDOW_ to all, so her Gate counts every return since S1, as DISTORTION does. Use *STEP* to advance. Most times Fee's needle steps, as the link from step 10 of [Some Notes on Procedure](/weblog/2026/11/22/some-notes-on-procedure.html) suggests, several of Flo's twelve synced source columns will also move noticeably. Like Flo, Fee shows readings, and every number carries its denominator: each block's printed marks pool runs at the dataset's baseline cell. The blocks keep the places their grouping gives them, by what a session receives. No session measures the crossing's implied rate, which comes from Black's form, borrowed for the letter. When synchronized, Fee and Flo read one tape at two scales: the exchange and the sources' returns. The 1.8 MB [zip](/weblog/assets/preterition-fee-flo.zip) carries Fee and Flo and plays from disk, and includes the binaries and data and code and license: I hope you'll feel free to tinker with it and poke at the internals---the code isn't minified---to see how it works.

Fee, Flo, and Zoe use mathematics to read a dataset comprising runs of sequentially composed machine prose. Ralph Hartley set meaning aside to measure information, and Claude Shannon, building on Hartley's work, called the semantic aspects of communication "irrelevant to the engineering problem" (1948, 379). The sentence often gets taken as a dismissal; I prefer to read it as modesty in the refusal to claim that a logarithm can capture meaning. Fee, Flo, and Zoe maintain the modesty: they count the material constraints that shape the sequential production of prose and leave the meaning to the reader.

Harold Black had his signed sketch witnessed. Fee, Flo, and Zoe ship with legible code and zipped data for the same reason: a reading enters the record when a witness repeats the measurement.

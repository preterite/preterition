In representations of the history of rhetoric, Quintilian often gets taught as an educational theorist. Quintilian should never, ever be read as an educational theorist without attention to his political context. (*This post synthesizes and extends [previous](/weblog/2006/04/15/why-we-need-tacitus.html) [thoughts](/weblog/2004/04/29/on-starting-tacitus.html) [from](/weblog/2004/05/03/oratory-and-terror.html) across a couple decades.*)

Scholars frequently misquote him removed from that political context: the *orator* must be a [*vir bonus dicendi peritus*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/12A*.html#1.1), a "good man, speaking well." The Latin is narrower, translating to "a good man skilled in speaking": a capacity. For Quintilian, the imagined *orator* was a role no longer practically available, and the exemplary qualities of the *vir bonus* were passed on by the *rhetor*, or what we would today call the professorial variant, a non-practicing scholar and teacher of rhetoric. The [cliché](/weblog/2026/10/09/reproduction-stock-and-flow.html) is to quote Quintilian for the good man speaking well, and to neglect its historical functional impossibility: we earnestly cite the figure of the good man speaking well as ethical model and miss its darkly exigent shadow. I indict the misuse of Quintilian as precedent, in the classical license composition cites when it argues for teaching writing as teaching character, and therefore that the writing teacher may claim jurisdiction over the student's interior.

<!--more-->

The phrase *vir bonus dicendi peritus* does not belong to Quintilian. Like Seneca the Elder, he cites it from Cato the Elder and Censor, also by the record an accuser, whose other famous phrase descends differently through history: *Carthago delenda est,* a modern Latin-textbook condensation and retrojection of [Pliny the Elder's description](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=15:chapter=20) of Cato *cum clamaret omni senatu Carthaginem delendam* ("when he would proclaim to all the senate that **Carthage must be destroyed**"). Quintilian's figure of the good man speaking well comes to us from the mouth of [the notorious early advocate for genocide](https://macmillan.yale.edu/sites/default/files/first_genocide.pdf). Under some emperors, Cato's name was dangerous, and the [*Dialogus de Oratoribus*](http://www.thelatinlibrary.com/tacitus/tac.dialogus.shtml) of Tacitus (which I've frequently extolled, and is one of the three texts I unfailingly assign in any history of rhetoric seminar, with Gorgias's *Encomium of Helen* and Plato's *Phaedrus*) opens on a poet who had offended the court with his recited tragedy *Cato*.

Quintilian was a *rhetor* appointed to the imperial court of Vespasian who went on to serve under the autocratic regime of the emperor Domitian. There was no place for the Ciceronian state-saving *orator* under the [Domitianic terror](https://www.societies.ncl.ac.uk/pgfnewcastle/files/2015/05/Eaton-Domitians-Reign-of-Terror.pdf), as all rhetorical deliberation previously worked through interaction and persuasion fell to the unitary discretion of the paranoid autocrat. Quintilian had not forgotten [Fulvia's piercing of Cicero's corpse's tongue with pins](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e8/Svedomsky-Fulvia.jpg), nor the display of his head and hands on the Rostra in the Forum. Citizens still sued each other over inheritances and property, and Quintilian's rockstar pupil, Pliny the Younger, made his career pleading cases in the centumviral courts, while the darker side of rhetorical practice promised other rewards.

As Tacitus documents, Domitian's terror was supported by a regime of professional accusers or [*delatores*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/311472) who claimed substantial portions of the executed estates of those they informed upon. Quintilian, briefly in [Book XII.7](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/12C*.html#7.3), declares the practices of professional accusers to be as despicable as robbery while praising accusation in the country's cause as heroic: one would think the consequences of a treason conviction rather worse than the loss of property; Tacitus, a senator under Domitian, acknowledged how ["our own hands dragged Helvidius to prison"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7524).

Quintilian then describes Domitius Afer, whom he had heard as a young man and who had made his name under Tiberius as an accuser, as the greatest orator he had known ([X.1.118](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/10A*.html#1.118); [XII.11.3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/12E*.html#11.3), where Afer's decline in old age becomes a lesson in knowing when to stop). Beyond petty civil litigation, the temporal horizon of rhetoric's deliberative orientation toward future practical action, as it had been practiced in Cicero's day, retreated to praise of the emperor's infallible decisions in the present. Rhetoric as theorized practice retreated to the only place it could: the classroom, where we find it in Quintilian's handbooks systematized into pedagogical prophylaxis from the effects of imperial power. 

Quintilian dedicates the twelve books of the [*Institutio Oratoria*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/home.html) or "Institutes of Oratory" to his friend Victorius Marcellus, for the education of Marcellus's son Geta. Before Quintilian, Seneca the Elder had documented the calibration of Roman rhetorical training toward a schoolhouse discipline in the *Suasoriae* and *Controversiae*, exercises for variation based upon templated and imagined examples, for the wealthy boys of the imperial elite to imitate and rehearse. Schoolboys advised Alexander and counseled Cicero on whether to burn his writings in exchange for his life, because they could never advise a Tiberius nor criticize a Domitian.

In [Book IX.2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/9B*.html#2.66), Quintilian names three occasions for figured speech: when it is "unsafe to speak openly," when it is "unseemly," and when it is "solely with a view to the elegance of what we say." The first is common in the schools, where students declaim against imagined tyrants and the decrees of a senate after civil war. Quintilian's overt advice suggests "we may speak against the tyrants in question as openly as we please . . . provided always that what we say is susceptible of a different interpretation." The tyrants in question are fictional. The business of the real courts "has never yet involved such necessity for silence," though a speaker may be "hampered by the existence of powerful personages, whom he must censure if he is to prove his case." Recall that Quintilian is writing under Domitian, whose regime Pliny the Younger, Tacitus, and Suetonius all characterized as terror. Shadi Bartsch's study of performative doublespeak under imperial surveillance, [*Actors in the Audience*](https://doi.org/10.4159/harvard.9780674280991), provides the proper register in which to read Quintilian's seemingly unselfconscious conclusion.

Additional impetus for the scoping of Quintilian's late-career curriculum came from a hyper-specific audience and a terrifyingly high-stakes project: Quintilian was personally appointed to the pedagogical preparation of Domitian's grandnephews, as the two newly named heirs of the emperor, for governing the entire Roman Empire. Quintilian's position as a salaried professor operating at the pinnacle of the Flavian state apparatus, and his subsequent position as the personal tutor for the imperial heirs, bent the spirit of his *magnum opus* toward the formation of an emperor. In such a context, Quintilian can be read as attempting [a desperate redefinition of what goodness means under a regime of terror](https://www.jstor.org/stable/298654). He seeks to create not a bold Ciceronian truth-teller, but a competent, eloquent bureaucrat who can navigate the imperial court without resorting to the murderous sycophancy of the *delatores*. Today, those might seem like the long-since-dissipated historical hopes for Obama-era models of the politician.

Modern educators look at [Book I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/1A*.html#1.4) of the *Institutio Oratoria* and see the meticulous analysis of how infants learn to speak and the cautions against nannies with bad accents as a general guide to childhood development. Contrary to that dehistoricized reading, we might consider that imperial heirs and the elites surrounding them could afford no public flaws, and an accent or stutter or other marker of human difference---as Claudius demonstrated---could weaken the emperor's projected façade of divine authority.

In IX.2, Quintilian offers a subtle inversion of the emperor's projection, noting that "our figures will have most effect upon the judge when he thinks that we use them with reluctance." The judge must believe he discovered the strategy independently, and must believe the speaker reveals by restraining the impulse to truth. Two staged interiors: the speaker's, performed as a suppressed impulse to truth, and the judge's, managed to disguise a conclusion engineered as a conclusion discovered. (The second is [the setup for Nolan's *Inception*](https://youtu.be/-LWJFwyy9Nw).)  The interior isn't so much inscribed[^1] as a surface built for another party's inference. The rhetorical effect of the *vir bonus* works the same way, even if not preceded by Quintilian's proper training: it succeeds when the judge thinks he sees it.

Writing teachers sometimes seem to want the figure of the *vir bonus* for its potential to reflect themselves in the *orator*. The [position](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html) we hold is not the *orator*'s but Quintilian's refiguring of that then-dead role, several steps down in status and compensation: the salaried *rhetor* teaching skills for deferred use, generalizable to constraints beyond the present classroom's.

Questions as to the ultimate success or failure of Quintilian's project must remain unanswered. In 95 CE, Domitian exiled the boys' mother and executed their father. The following year, [the emperor's steward distracted him](https://doi.org/10.29228/joh.50649) by handing him a paper to read, and pulled a concealed dagger to stab him in the groin. Domitian fought back and gouged out the steward's eyes. Other assassins joined and stabbed the emperor seven more times before he died.

The boys vanish from the record.


[^1]: The contextually anachronistic use of the technological terms "inscribed," "surface," "inference," "training," and "record" is intentional.

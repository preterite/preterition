### Hardest working

I've spent recent posts building an account of writing's economy. In my 2014 "[Economies of Writing, Without the Economics](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350198.2014.917514)" article, I argued that composition's economic discourse both represented the economy as a force beyond intervention and borrowed economic terms as metaphors for non-economic exchange. I concluded by defining economic activity as "the appropriation of value at any stage in the ... cycle of production, distribution, use, and re-production" (Edwards 2014, 258), and by looking toward an account grounded in economic scholarship that would trace writing's value in movement beyond the market. That account, presented in the series this post concludes, defines value and identifies who appropriates what value from writing, as artifact, activity, and system, and when and how they do so. I've held off more deeply engaging [Asao Inoue](https://doi.org/10.37514/PER-B.2022.1824)'s work until now, because his most closely meshes with the spirit here---in what follows, I treat Inoue as an ally---and the distinctions that remain clarify both our approaches.

Inoue's labor-based grading contracts foreground the economic valuation of student work, particularly as located in the nexus of the grade. Inoue first removes the teacher's judgment of quality from the grade, because "all grading and assessment exist within systems that uphold singular, dominant standards that are racist, and white supremacist when used uniformly" (Inoue 2022, 3). I agree, and a manuscript I'm working on examines composition's nineteenth-century disciplinary development and its deep connections to the economics of white supremacy and coerced labor. The grade Inoue's contract computes attempts to evacuate any valuation based upon white supremacist standardized assessments of writing's quality, and to instead count the student's labor. Grading to a standard grades to the history of the standard, and the student has no hand in the standard's coercive history.

Students and teachers bring their own economic histories. I attended three undergrad institutions and incorporated the economic differences I saw among them into my emerging sense of competing valuations. My military experience as an enlisted soldier who signed up for the GI Bill, and later as a deployed Department of the Army civilian, sharpened my sense of the economic inequalities associated with writing's technologies. Inoue's experience similarly informs his economics: "I may not be the smartest guy in the room, but I damn well will be the hardest working one," he told himself upon entering college, and he "leaned heavily on the doing of things" because he "couldn't always depend on how others would judge the products" of his labor (2022, 15). The grade gets produced in the gap between the writer's count and another party's valuation.

Three questions follow the grade from the site where its work was done: why it was made, when it counts, and to what end. Five years ago, [I was reading Inoue's book with Ricardo and Sraffa in mind](/weblog/2021/05/10/contracts-and-capital.html), and developing ideas about the nineteenth-century origins of composition's measures of economic valuation. Some of that thinking led to the six laws I've formulated in this series.

### The six laws

For reference, the six laws follow. Each takes up the last clause of the preceding law. The first law proceeds from the law that closed the series on technology, and the sixth law's final clause loops back to the first law.

[Transformation: Storage and Time](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html): Stored labor becomes capital only in a relation, at a use.

1. [Valuation: Labor and Measure](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html).
<span id="law-1-1">(1.1)</span> Taking up a use is a valuation, and valuations vary with uses.
<span id="law-1-2">(1.2)</span> Value is the labor time a society must spend, which a machine can count and no machine can take up.
<span id="law-1-3">(1.3)</span> The line between what is counted and what is judged falls where a society stops being willing to spend, drawn by a party with a stake.

2. [Appropriation: Position and Relation](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html).
<span id="law-2-1">(2.1)</span> A party with a stake is a position in a relation, which persons occupy and leave.
<span id="law-2-2">(2.2)</span> The surplus made at any stage of the economic cycle is appropriated at that stage by whoever holds the position to do so, the maker included, and by more than one party, since a text is not used up in the taking.
<span id="law-2-3">(2.3)</span> The share a maker takes in the making, where it is realized, no ledger can reach.

3. [Commensuration: Text and Labor](/weblog/2026/10/05/commensuration-text-and-labor.html).
<span id="law-3-1">(3.1)</span> A ledger reaches only what it can commensurate.
<span id="law-3-2">(3.2)</span> Whoever holds it sets the unlike labor of different writers equal at a ratio the writers can neither see set nor contest.
<span id="law-3-3">(3.3)</span> The holder attributes that ratio to a product, which stands in for the labor no copy carries.

4. [Aggregation: Value and Price](/weblog/2026/10/06/aggregation-value-and-price.html).
<span id="law-4-1">(4.1)</span> A ratio attributed to a product is a price.
<span id="law-4-2">(4.2)</span> Price settles by a snapshot, setting unlike capital and the two accounts of value equal at an instant, and weighting labor of every date by that instant's distribution.
<span id="law-4-3">(4.3)</span> What the snapshot settles at an instant, labor settles only over time.

5. [Capitalization: Title and Rent](/weblog/2026/10/07/capitalization-title-and-rent.html).
<span id="law-5-1">(5.1)</span> What labor settles only over time, a title claims as rent on position.
<span id="law-5-2">(5.2)</span> Whoever buys or holds the title prices that rent at once, capitalizing it at the going rate of interest, and the price appears as the value of the stock, whatever labor the stock stores.
<span id="law-5-3">(5.3)</span> Since a reading uses up nothing, the holder sells the stock again wherever the title can exclude a buyer, and a stock priced before anyone takes it up presumes its realization.

6. [Reproduction: Stock and Flow](/weblog/2026/10/09/reproduction-stock-and-flow.html).
<span id="law-6-1">(6.1)</span> A taking-up realizes what a stock priced before anyone takes it up presumes, and the same count reads as record or forecast by the position that takes it up.
<span id="law-6-2">(6.2)</span> That presumption is reproduced at each use where a party with a stake takes it up, whatever readings arrive.
<span id="law-6-3">(6.3)</span> The taking-up that realizes value, booked or unbooked, re-produces the stock it draws on, since taking up a use is a valuation.

### Counted everywhere except

I'll open my recap with the credit. [The holder of a ledger sets the unlike labor of different writers equal at a ratio the writers can neither see set nor contest (3.2)](/weblog/2026/10/05/commensuration-text-and-labor.html). Inoue's contract publicly sets that ratio at 1:1 and negotiates the ratio with the writers it will count. An hour of any student's labor counts as an hour, five hundred words as five hundred words, the class agrees to the terms at the semester's outset, and "student-laborers must participate in determining the exchange-value of their labor" (Inoue 2022, 102). The writer counted by the contract becomes a party with a stake, drawing the line between what is counted and what is judged (1.3). One hour producing more than another is "only unfair if" the economy's purpose is to accumulate exchange-value "and if there is an agreed-upon standard by which to judge the products of labor" (Inoue 2022, 98). Inoue's contract declines that commodifying standard.

Position grants significance. A party with a stake is a position in a relation, which persons occupy and leave (2.1). The student occupies more positions at once than anyone else in the economic cycle represented in [Cohle's Pinwheel](/weblog/assets/cycle-figure.svg). Susan Miller's "preeconomic" (1991, 87) student is [imagined by the discipline as standing outside valued economic activity](/weblog/2026/09/20/forgone-optimization.html). In *[The Wealth of Nations](https://archive.org/details/inquiryintonatur01smit_0)*, Adam Smith counts the student everywhere except as labor: the person educated at cost figures as an "expensive machine" whose investment must return with a profit (1776, I.x.b.6), and the student pays the teacher for the lecture (1776, V.i.f.1), so the student shows up in the ledger as an invested sum and a paying customer, never as the one whose hours the course consumes. The student carries the total of all positions. She pays, she produces, she lands the investment; the certificate names her as its product, the course works her as its raw material; and each position reads her labor on its own terms: the payer's as a purchase, the investor's as a deposit, the product's as a yield. None of Smith's positions reads the student as a productive laborer. Inoue's contract grants the student the laborer's position withheld by Smith's bundle, and Inoue's phrase "participate in determining" identifies the position.

Erika Lindemann's [handbook for new writing teachers](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/0195130456) describes the mechanics of such student positioning. In Lindemann's account, student-generated criteria let the class rather than the teacher set the standard, but "the hidden agenda, of course, is that students will develop criteria similar to those we'd use anyway" (2001, 209). The teacher, as holder of the ledger, sets the ratio (3.2). Lindemann's class negotiates the criteria for quality---the features a composition must demonstrate to earn the grade---and the negotiation ends where the teacher's standard already stood. The teacher partly steers the negotiation, the students seeking the grade partly steer themselves, and the standard enters the grade through the criteria the class believes it wrote.

Inoue's contract negotiates a different object. The class sets the count: the hours and the terms for recording them. No judgment of a paper's quality enters the tabulation, so the teacher's standard has no access to the number; that channel, from standard to criteria to grade, closes at the point where the grade is computed. It stays open everywhere else in the classroom. The teacher reads the drafts and responds, and Inoue retains those readings as carriers of economic value, since "it is the judgments and feedback that have exchange- and use-value" (2022, 85). The standard reaches the student through the feedback, and through the student's wish to please whoever gives it, but stops short of the grade.

### Commodification revisited

A process, once assessed, becomes product. Ask a class to think critically and grade the thinking, and "critical thinking" gets commodified into agreeing with the grader, the criterion masquerading as the act it names. Hours are more difficult to counterfeit than agreement. In assessment, the circulation of economic value "usually is linked to desires for high grades and desires to please the teacher" (Inoue 2022, 91). The desire to please reaches a labor log as readily as it reaches an essay. Inoue's contract declines to grade by standards exterior to the classroom. In economic terms, a standard holds its authority as rent on position (5.1), collected at a focal point: [an academic journal's prestige, following Bergstrom, operates as a rent on a meeting place](/weblog/2026/10/07/capitalization-title-and-rent.html). A writing standard collects the same rent, with experienced teachers who "seem to have internalized the criteria" for A through F papers (Lindemann 2001, 226 n.2) as lore, held at the focal point where the graders meet. Inoue's contract leaves that rent uncollected.

I've proposed [contracting on what a corpus shows](/weblog/2026/09/15/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-3.html): its volume, the structure of the connections among its pieces, and a human judgment of how a writer represents herself in it; descriptors rather than rankings or scores. Under the six laws, my contract touches the product: volume and the structure of connections belong to the student's writing, and a ratio attributed to those qualities attaches to a product (3.3). Inoue locates the white supremacist harms of grading in the judgment of the text produced against a standard. His count engages the product as quantity, words or pages counted, never as a ratio to that standard.

Our contracts rest on different principles, each with a consequence absent from the other: Inoue contracts on labor, the hours a student spends, and stops at quantity; my contract on the product reaches the text---the corpus a student has written and its connections---as a ratio, where Inoue locates the harm. In Part 3 of the [Embodied Agnostic Subject series](/series) I called the difference "time-as-measure against product-and-relation-as-measure"; Lindemann had drawn the same distinction in 2001, between kinds of writing course. For Lindemann, a "what-centered course" has a subject matter, or what she says James Moffett called the "it" of a course: the content a student seeks to learn. Lindemann notes that such content can be "expressed as nouns" (Lindemann 2001, 258). A process-centered course has no "it" of that variety; instead, its content is what the student does with language, and such a course is "characterized by verbs" (258). Inoue's contract grades the verb in the hours of doing, while mine grades the noun as the corpus written. [Writing operates as both noun and verb](/weblog/2026/09/27/composition-noun-and-verb.html). Inoue's contract and my contract each reach a portion of writing's value; neither adequately accounts for its entirety.

### Hardest working, decomposed

Discard the standard, and the grade still requires a measure. That measure, for Inoue, is the hour: "For the purposes of course grading, everyone's time is of equal exchange-value in the assessment economy, even though that same labor's use-value or worth may not be measured the same" (Inoue 2022, 101). Inoue reports that Adam Smith set hours aside as "too abstract," since skill and soil vary too much for one hour to equal another (2022, 96). Inoue nonetheless retains the hour, crediting the uniform standard for exchange-value to both his sources as "what Marx and Smith understood" (2022, 98). I don't take the crediting as a confusion. My first law's second clause defines value, from [Marx](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch01.htm#S1), as [the labor time a society must spend](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html) (1.2): the weaver's hour is counted at the social average rather than at her loom. Inoue counts the individual hour, Smith's equal quantities rather than Marx's necessary time, because the classroom aims at "worth," which "cannot have a uniform standard" (2022, 98). The uniform standard goes to the grade, where a class can agree to the individual hour, and the grade stops claiming what a standard would claim.

How, then, does Inoue's notion of "hardest working" complicate the analysis? What are the components of hard work? In Marx's terms, "hard work" decomposes into duration (the measured hour), intensity (how much effort went into the hour), and productivity (what the hour yielded) (Marx 1992, 655). A log counts the duration, a standard assesses the productivity, and intensity calls on what no count reaches: the interior of the subject. The credit hour fixes the duration, a working day of set length, [traced to the plan that set it](/weblog/2026/09/22/certification-by-plan.html). Inoue's contract adopted a unit the plan had already cut.

By Marx's reasoning, individual differences in labor, or "'errors' as they are called in mathematics, compensate each other and vanish whenever a certain minimum number of workers are employed together" (Marx 1992, 440): "average social quality" belongs to the workforce rather than the individual worker, and the party that holds the average holds the measure. An individual hour can stand in for the average only at scale. The student holds the hour and no average; the teacher holds the class, where the differences begin to cancel; the authors of assessment standards hold the population, where the differences disappear. Inoue's contract counts at student scale and admits the ratio at the teacher's scale---the smallest scale at which an average can exist.

Our disciplinary understandings of value's relation to assessment are sometimes historically elided. Lindemann's work, like composition's general orientation toward assessment, owes a debt we no longer much consider to the earlier work of scholars like Charles Cooper and Lee Odell. In Cooper and Odell's 1977 collection *[Evaluating Writing](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED143020)*, Mary Beaven sought the hour of work with no grade attached: her self-evaluation questions to students open with "How much time did you spend on this paper?" (Beaven 1977, 143), and one of her students answered the question with guilt, "because the actual writing went as smoothly and rapidly and I know others in the class spent hours" (144). The student's answer measures intensity: a question about time answered from the interior. Hours compared across a class carry valuations no grade records. Assignments that imply one theory of value and grade by another, according to [Richard Fulkerson](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc197916201), perform a "value-mode confusion" (1979, 347); far preferable would be keeping a notion of "worth" separate from a grade's theory of value. Inoue constructs the contract to avoid the confusion, taking the grade from the labor theory of value it implies. Worth never takes a grade.

### How a count presents

In the classroom, the count presents as a count. Inoue's log counts how much, and the number offers "a simple binary judgment of fact (not value): did the student complete the labor task asked of them?" (2022, 115). I've suggested that [the dangerous case runs in the other direction](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html): a count presented as a valuation, the similarity score taken as an assessment of the writer. My 2014 article counted the *JAC* issue's sources authored by economists or indexed by EconLit, characterizing the tally as "a crude heuristic" (Edwards 2014, 251). I allowed for "some value in counting things" (Edwards 2014, 254), and noted that academics use "citation counts and journal impact evaluations" for tenure and promotion (Edwards 2014, 259 n.3). My drawing on that count as evidence of engagement, even with the count presented only as a count, pulled me toward the case I later described as dangerous. Inoue's log makes the dangerous case honest: a count that presents as a count. It reaches only "time on tasks, number of words or pages" (Inoue 2022, 105), duration and quantity, and makes no claim about quality.

The student dictates where the count stops. Students ask Inoue whether an hour's break spent still considering the reading counts, or whether the drive home spent cogitating through a draft counts as class labor. Inoue says he's "always left these questions up to the student to decide and reflect upon in labor journals" (2022, 113). A party with a stake draws the line between what is counted and what is judged (1.3), and the contract places the delimiter in the hand of the person to whom the hours belong. Inoue's contract carries a consequence mine lacks: the writer's labor takes its boundary from the writer.

Inoue's log also records what it does not count. Beside the hours recorded rest "engagement ratings on individual labor sessions" (2022, 104), the student's reading of how hard the hours went, so the student inscribes ungraded intensity: the work's witness makes the interior call, while the tabulation excludes that call. Some hours cannot go faster. Inoue takes from Zajonc the line that "one cannot meditate fast" (119), and I see in it an analogue to [Baumol and Bowen](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1816292)'s violinists: "The output per man-hour of the violinist playing a Schubert quartet in a standard concert hall is relatively fixed" (Baumol and Bowen 1965, 500). The log's refusal to grade intensity says a slow hour counts as an hour.

### Value and worth

A student writer is a writer. When I teach first-year composition, I open the semester with a forecast about worth. Nobody demands that you enter the class as a good writer, I say; the course operates to help you become a better one. Some students may feel like they're skilled writers; others, less so. If you do the work, you'll leave a better writer than you entered. Learning happens in the doing, and the learning belongs to you: I can't do the learning for you any more than I can do the writing. The course can help make the boring parts more efficient, and offer you space to suit the work of writing to your needs. The school wants its assessed share from your writing, and the school's share comes easily. Making the writing worthwhile for yourself, rather than a grade for someone else, is harder.

Teachers know that teaching is partly (mostly?) persuasion. I sometimes worry my brief speech promises more than any count can back. A forecast about what a student will take from the doing claims a share no ledger reaches (2.3), a share the clause can request and never verify. I make the request on day one of the semester. Inoue does the same: the labor-based contract system "requires students to have some initial faith ... and experience it for a while before they realize and feel what benefits there are for them" (Inoue 2022, 115).

[Pierre Bourdieu](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/fr/bourdieu-forms-capital.htm) categorizes such promises as investment. Education's profits arrive "in a deferred, indirect way" (Bourdieu 1986, 244) distinct from [those theorized by human-capital economists](/weblog/2026/10/09/reproduction-stock-and-flow.html). For Bourdieu, the student's gain from the doing operates as a stock to be drawn upon later. The time taken at the laboring moment gets converted into a return that manifests---if at all---in the economist's long run.

The student reports upon exit. Worth, for Inoue, "is measured in desire, not in money" (2022, 87), a quality "felt and understood through reflections and metacognitive work" (94) at midterm and final. A labor-based economy accumulates "individual worth, since it embodies what one has learned and what one can transfer after the course is over" (Inoue 2022, 101). Inoue's language carries two separable readings of worth. Worth felt in the doing belongs to the share a maker takes in the making, where it is realized, which no ledger reaches (2.3). Inoue's log counts among those ledgers: the reflection reports a realization it cannot deliver. On the other hand, the notion of worth as transferable after course completion aligns with Bourdieu's deferred investment: a stock kept for later, realized---if at all---at another position's taking-up.

I part from Bourdieu at the point of investment, and the difference carries consequence for self-appropriated value. My second law says the maker appropriates the share in the doing, and declines to say whether the doing was disinterested. Bourdieu reads the disinterested share as a deferred investment, and allows that "the most sincerely disinterested acts may be those best corresponding to objective interest" (1986, 254). Inoue's text supports both readings, but the classroom determines neither: a position decides later, at a taking-up.

### Count zero interrupt

In the classroom the grade serves as a count, presented as a count. On the transcript, the grade serves as a number next to a course designation, and [the party that takes up the outbound grade](/weblog/2026/10/09/reproduction-stock-and-flow.html) reads it as record or forecast from the position that takes it up (6.1). Inoue characterizes the transcript-based exchangeability of grades in their nature as "indicators of use, of learning, and thus of what the student offers future employers or graduate programs, or [as] some predictor of future success" (Inoue 2022, 85): his account has the employer read the grade as a forecast. His contract reaches no employer.

Cooper and Odell open *Evaluating Writing* with the uses to which evaluating writing may be put. Administrative uses top their list: prediction, placement, and "public letter or number grades" (Cooper and Odell 1977, ix). A measure with predictive validity "predicts performance at some time in the future or it correlates well with some criterion, say grades earned in English" (xi), with predictive validity being "of interest primarily to makers of standardized tests" (xii). Teachers and researchers, on the other hand, attend to content and construct. Cooper and Odell's list sorts the evaluation by the positions that take it up: the same number predicts for whoever admits, places for whoever assigns sections, and stands as a public grade for whoever keeps the transcript. The editors ask that their readers know which position before choosing a measure.

[Fulkerson](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc19908950) offers an example of how the assigner of the grade holds a position. When the student readers whom a writer named as her audience "affirm that they find a paper very effective," Fulkerson wants to object that those readers should not have done so, having "really judged the paper inadequate from a mimetic point of view" because the paper rests on a false premise about parking spaces or nonresident tuition (1990, 422)---but the teacher and not the audience addressed assigns the grade (423). The grade carries one valuation, made in the classroom from one position. The paper's audience never holds the grading position. For Bourdieu, "educational qualifications never function perfectly as currency," because "their value rises in proportion to the value of their bearer" (1986, 248). The number departs the classroom with its bearer, and varies with its bearer.

The contract's reach extends no further than its signatures. Exchange requires every party to consent, and Inoue's contract is "set up and primarily controlled not by the teacher, but by students and teacher" (2022, 92). The laborers who sign the contract "participate in determining the exchange-value of their labor" (Inoue 2022, 102). Neither the employer nor the registrar signs. Adding another party's signature later does not extend the contract, since any taking-up beyond the present becomes a separate exchange with different parties. The limit lies in scope: a contract resets the ratio at the signing position. The positions beyond read the grade on their own terms, each taking up the number as the record or forecast required by its office.

The limit determines what each position can do. The teacher resets the ratio where she signs, and the contract inscribes the terms: what the number counts (hours and words) versus what it does not (the quality of the writing). The transcript records the number without the terms. The student holds the log as the dated account of the hours no transcript can carry. The reader beyond---an employer, a committee---can ask whether the grade records labor done or forecasts work to come, and who signed its terms. The number answers neither question; the contract answers both. Each act stops at the position that performs it: the syllabus binds the course, the log stays with the student, the query remains with its querent.

### Valuation's locations recycled

An economy requires a subject. At the outset of this series, I compared the permissions of [various access regimes](/weblog/2026/10/02/access-clauses-elaborated.html): what may be known about the agent, from what evidence, at what moment, by whom, and what the regime claims to reach. The six laws describe positions. The comparison table from that post (Marx and Smith remain unchanged; I've added Bourdieu and Inoue, and my embodied agnostic subject) outlines what each regime can know of the person in the position.

| Theorist (tradition) | What may be known about the agent | From what evidence | At what moment | By whom | What the regime claims to reach |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adam Smith (classical; labor commanded) | The laborer's sacrifice of ease, liberty and happiness, the same for every laborer | The toil and trouble of acquiring a thing | While the labor is done | The theorist, on every laborer's behalf | The interior |
| Karl Marx (labor theory of value; socially necessary labor time) | A capacity, labor-power, bought in open exchange and used up in production; what the laborer feels or thinks doesn't matter to capital | The wage contract and the working day; socially necessary labor time | Over the working day, in time | Whoever holds the record of the relation | The record only |
| Pierre Bourdieu (forms of capital; habitus) | Capital as accumulated labor, appropriated on an exclusive basis and appropriating social energy as reified or living labor; the agent's incorporated dispositions and their class history, a frozen moment accumulated by history | Surveyed practices and tastes, summed at the going conversion rates, a [snapshot](/weblog/2026/10/06/aggregation-value-and-price.html) | At the survey's moment, with history read back | The sociologist, while the agents misrecognize | The interior and its cause, from the act |
| Asao Inoue, the grade (labor-based grading contract) | Hours and words declared | The labor log | At the semester's close | The class, under its agreed standard | The record only |
| Asao Inoue, worth (desire after Lacan, Veblen and Bourdieu) | What the labor means to the student | Reflections at midterm and final | At midterm and final | The student, about herself | The interior, by self-report |
| the embodied agnostic subject (Butler, Glissant, Marx) | Nothing about the interior; the record of labor and of its uses | The record | Over time, as the record accumulates | Whoever holds the record; no one, about the interior | The record only |

Inoue's row for worth lines up with [Peter Elbow's](/weblog/2026/10/02/access-clauses-elaborated.html) in the evidence it admits. The interior enters only as testimony: no party save the writer can check the report against the labor it measures, and the regime reaches the interior by crediting the student's testimony. Inoue's row for the grade aligns with the readers of the record, Marx's and my own, as hours declared and no claim beyond the record. The two rows disagree over what the course reaches because the instruments themselves disagree: the log admits hours and claims the record, while the reflection admits testimony and claims the interior. Inoue runs both instruments. [I earlier aligned Inoue with the embodied agnostic subject](/weblog/2026/09/15/the-embodied-agnostic-subject-part-3.html), suggesting that neither claims to read an interior. In making that claim, I considered the log and not the reflection. Inoue's grade reads only the record, and his worth reaches the interior through the student's report. My counter-measure, a contract on the corpus, reads the record alongside Inoue's grade and reaches no further than the text produced.

The positions entail the demand for the number. Beaven observed that teachers who stop grading feel they are "not giving students what they deserve and what taxes are paid for," and that students left to evaluate themselves begin "begging teachers to assume the responsibility and give them grades" (1977, 147). The teacher's position carries an obligation (both to the student and to the public paying the taxes) she believes the number discharges. The student's position carries a responsibility she wants reclaimed by the teacher. The hours recorded by the number play no part in either demand.

### Writing's diverse economy

The student's log records the dates. Every hour in the log belongs to the person who spent it, and the log reaches the labor as a count of hours and words with dates attached. Lindemann describes grading as "a closed procedure," a judgment made "in ways that further revision can't change" (Lindemann 2001, 226). The grade the log produces closes the same way: on the day the semester ends, a number with a date attached. My laws trace a ring, the sixth's last clause handing back to the first, and the grade runs the ring: counted at one date, taken up at another, valued again at each taking-up, since each use is a valuation. The positions beyond the semester wait to value the grade's number. The transcript travels with the date and without the contract's terms; each taking-up of the transcript goes undated until it occurs. The contract keeps its dates.

Every writer keeps their dates as well: drafts saved, posts archived, an article cited years later, a corpus licensed to train a model. Each instrument that values the writing (a grade, a citation count, a publisher's licensing fee) reaches a portion of the writing, at a ratio set by whoever holds the instrument. Most ratios get set where writers can neither see them set nor contest them. A labor-based contract sets its ratio in public with the writers it counts, and the classroom becomes the site my article sought, where writers openly exercise economic agency (Edwards 2014, 245).

The rest of writing's economy operates at larger scales and scopes. In [Gibson-Graham's iceberg](https://www.communityeconomies.org/resources/diverse-economies-iceberg), the typical understanding of an economy as "wage labor, market exchange of commodities, and capitalist enterprise" makes up "but a small subset of the activities by which we produce, exchange, and distribute values" (2006, 69). The unpriced takings-up of writing hide below the waterline: a reader thinks through an uncited essay, or a writer returns to her own archive and restarts [the economic cycle](/weblog/assets/cycle-figure.svg). The takings-up realize value whether or not the writer records the realization (6.3). Among the takings-up still undated, one remains with the writer, who retains the dates and the terms of her labor, and provisions herself from her record.

The activities of writing's economy---enclosed by no classroom, priced by no market---take place over time: writing requires labor and produces value only as it moves through the cycle of production, distribution, use, and re-production. Dates mark the time over which the value of writing's diverse labor inputs accumulates.

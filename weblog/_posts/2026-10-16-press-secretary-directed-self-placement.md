Let us now praise the skipper of the Disneyland Jungle Cruise. The skipper must skillfully pantomime steering, spinning the wooden wheel and gazing intently toward the perils of the water passage ahead, while the boat judders and clunks along its fixed subfluvial track. The skipper must narrate the travel, synchronizing the memorized speech to the expected progress through the stations. The skipper must dramatize the surrounding peril while reassuring the audience of their safety. And the skipper's delivery must be flawlessly deadpan, no matter how counterfeit the narrated account of the travel and its scenery may seem.

There can be no finer patron saint for the White House Press Secretary position than the Jungle Cruise skipper Ron Ziegler. Ziegler was not an attorney, nor did he need or pretend to be one. He worked at Disneyland for two years as a USC undergraduate before serving as a press aide on Richard Nixon's failed 1962 gubernatorial campaign. H. R. Haldeman, the campaign's manager, hired him at the J. Walter Thompson agency's L.A. office and took him along to Nixon's 1968 run. In the deepening crises of Nixon's imperial presidency, Ziegler admitted that his previous statements as Press Secretary were "inoperative": the ride was broken, the skipper done.

The [headlines about the five-day reign of Katie Zacharia](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/us/politics/katie-zacharia-fired-press-secretary.html) in the role have me wondering what the Mouth of the Presidency should do, and what follows when someone seeking entry to a guild publicly fails a high-stakes writing test.

<!--more-->

There's no historical need or requirement or precedent, as Ziegler's example demonstrates, that a White House Press Secretary be an attorney. The mistake was in the claiming and the cover-up: Katie Zacharia's role had no requirement that she claim the authority of being an attorney. Nor did its practice require the precision of an attorney: a Mouth of the Presidency's role, by its nature, requires persuasive imprecision and plausible deniability. One wants the fast responses and message management of a campaign operative like Psaki or professional communicator like Perino, and one wants no Press Secretary's words anywhere near legal bindability. So Katie Zacharia made an *ethos* claim that neither aspect of the position required, and some jurisdictions consider such claims, if falsely made, to be criminal concerns.

The unmentioned shame under the claim is a failed bar exam: a high-stakes writing test at the door of a guild. That sent me poking. For most descriptions of the bar I could find, half the score comes from the Multistate Bar Examination, 200 multiple-choice questions whose raw scores get statistically adjusted onto a 200-point scale. Practicing attorneys and judges grade the written half with 1-to-6 or 1-to-10 rubrics. Then the examiners scale the essays to the bubbles, shifting the written scores until their mean and standard deviation match the cohort’s multiple-choice scores. The essays derive a scaled score from the bubbles, and the two adjusted scores are summed. Anyone who's spent summers reading placement essays may find both the scoring and the labor arrangement unusual.

Composition’s literature doesn't offer much on the bar examination. One small lead: [a 1959 argument in _College Composition and Communication_](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc195922180) for a credentialing exam for NCTE membership, titled “A Proposal for ‘Bar Exams,’” from some guy named Richard Braddock. Nobody licenses the title “writing teacher”---though I wouldn't mind the opportunity to flip open a wallet and flash the ID and say, "It's OK, citizen: I'm a *writing teacher*." California licenses “lawyer”: the regulated act includes the identity claim. Zacharia’s Instagram bio reads “Wife, Mom, & Lawyer.” All three nouns entail state documentation; an unauthorized claim on the third may violate the law.

Writing programs and writing teachers using Directed Self-Placement provide students with course descriptions and questions about their reading and writing, then help the students place themselves. [The premise](https://www.jstor.org/stable/358352): the writer knows better than a timed test. By that standard, “I did lawyer stuff in Beijing, so I’m a lawyer” indicates perfectly good self-placement for a credential the job never required. Such complexities evaporate when the director directing the directed self-placement---audience and boss---is the current imperial President.

A few weeks ago, I netstumbled again upon [Amazon's Mechanical Turk](http://www.mturk.com/mturk/welcome),  a for-hire [crowdsourcing system](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowdsourcing) that I remember causing a brief buzz when it came out in 2005 or 2006. I was deep in dissertation tunnel vision at the time, not wanting to let myself be distracted, but I remember thinking it held interesting possibilities as a highly decentralized market for [immaterial labor](/weblog/2006/02/01/a-digital-working-class.html), and wondered how it might connect to what I'd been saying about the economics of writing.

So I've finally caught a short breather from the end-of-the-semester crunch -- I'm presently sitting in the hall while my plebes are about 25 minutes into taking their term-end examinations and typing busily away -- and did some poking around. [Interesting stuff](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Mechanical_Turk). The [job requester](http://requester.mturk.com/mturk/welcome) command-line interface stuff is a little daunting, but on the worker side, there are -- as of this morning -- 111 [jobs available](http://www.mturk.com/mturk/searchbar?selectedSearchType=hitgroups&searchWords=write&minReward=0.00&x=8&y=14&=%2Fsearchbar) with the keyword "write" in the listing. Which made me wonder once more: [how much should you pay for a C+ paper?](/weblog/2005/03/18/4cs-owning-knowledge.html)

Or, OK, to be a little less opaque about it: the Amazon Mechanical Turk offers one system of thinking about the value of what they call Human Intelligence Tasks. In looking over those Human Intelligence Tasks, I think they're certainly a form of Hardt and Negri's immaterial labor, but of a somewhat different order than, say, writing an essay. Yet some of them -- e.g., writing reviews -- come close to the types of low-stakes tasks we sometimes assign in FYC. And "stakes" is yet another term related to value.

Curious. Further investigation needed.

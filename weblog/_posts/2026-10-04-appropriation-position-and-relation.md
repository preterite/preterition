### Under the cloak

I first heard the familiar joke about Plato's *Phaedrus* from a classicist. Socrates encounters Phaedrus headed for a stroll beyond the city wall ([Plato 1914](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0174%3Atext%3DPhaedrus), 227a). Phaedrus has spent the midsummer morning listening to Lysias read a speech about love (228b). Socrates flirts with "the fair young Phaedrus" (261a) as they walk the banks of the Ilissus. Lysias argued that a young man should grant his favors to a man who doesn't love him, and Socrates, sensing that Phaedrus carries a copy of the speech (228d), demands to hear it: "Is that a scroll under your cloak, Phaedrus, or are you just happy to see me?"

The day's heat shapes the unfolding of the flirtatious exchange. Among the reasons Phaedrus offers secondhand from Lysias for favoring the non-lover is a metaphorical ledger: favors should go to those best able to repay them (233e); the scroll, as a commodity, argues for exchange. Lysias writes speeches by trade (257c), what we might today call advocacy or ghostwriting or plagiarism, depending on context: he composes for clients in the courtroom who deliver his sentences as their own. The client takes the authorship with the text.

<!--more-->

Later in the dialogue, Socrates relates an origin myth. Theuth, the Egyptian god and inventor of writing and other arts, brings his inventions to the king Thamus, whom the Greeks call Ammon (274d), and commends writing as a means to memory and to wisdom (274e). Thamus answers that "one man has the ability to beget arts, but the ability to judge of their usefulness or harmfulness to their users belongs to another" (274e--275a). Writing, the king says, is a means of reminding, not memory (275a). [Eric Schliesser](https://crookedtimber.org/2026/06/17/on-the-governance-of-llms-and-the-university-of-chicago/) read the story this summer as a governance parable: Theuth invents, Ammon decides whether the invention circulates, the inventor misjudges the invention. In "Plato's Pharmacy," [Jacques Derrida](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/0485300052) sees the scene as a site of valuation, in that "the value of writing will not be itself, writing will have no value, unless and to the extent that god-the-king approves of it" (Derrida 1981, 76). The king sets the price of what he receives, and from that position he discounts the gift. Plato, who inscribes the king's warning and sends Phaedrus with a scroll to tempt Socrates (230d--e), seems to me more ambivalent than naïve about the technology.

Thamus apportions begetting to one party and judging to another, as does the dialogue entire. Lysias writes, Phaedrus borrows (228b), Socrates responds; a client delivers, and the king sets the price. The value, as Derrida's king demonstrates, isn't in the text. (Is it, then, outside the text?) It's set in a relation, by the party holding the position to set it, and parties take from the text at different points in passage, exhausting it at none.

I [closed a 2003 entry](/weblog/2003/08/01/history-technology-phaedrus.html) by calling for another reading of the *Phaedrus*. [The following night](/weblog/2003/08/02/mechanisms-for-forgetting.html) I wrote that writing had, as Socrates warned, "become a mechanism for forgetting; we forgot even his warning." Turns out I forgot the warning for umm lessee *&lt;checks watch&gt;* a couple decades and change? Maybe now's as good a time as any to close that loop and ask of the dialogue my last post's [closing question](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html): *who holds the position, and who captures the surplus?*

### A position, not a person

[A party with a stake is a position in a relation, which persons occupy and leave](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html). Phaedrus is a borrower in the morning and a declaimer by afternoon. Lysias writes for a client and holds the authorship until the client delivers. Thamus judges an art he can't practice. They do not maintain positions.

[Resnick and Wolff](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html) build their class analysis on position. *Transformation* used Resnick and Wolff's concept of class as a process rather than a group: the performing, appropriating, and distributing of surplus labor. For Resnick and Wolff, a fundamental class process has performers and appropriators of surplus. A subsumed class process, on the other hand, distributes surplus already appropriated to the party supplying the condition the appropriation requires; it has positions of its own, a distributor and a recipient, and "such a position occurs within (is a personification of) a subsumed class process" (Resnick and Wolff 1987, 118). A personification is a role: the same person can perform surplus labor in the morning and receive a share of someone else's in the afternoon, and the record shows which position she stood in at each hour without reporting anything from the interior. Althusser's overdetermination implies that a position arrives with the relation that requires it and departs when the relation does. The law's first clause asks no more: to find who takes value at a stage, find the relation, find its positions, and find who was standing in each when the surplus was appropriated.

### The cycle, circulated

In 2007, preparing a CCCC talk, [I set myself](/weblog/2007/02/21/739.html) the task of producing "a basic and rudimentary rhetoric of the process of production, appropriation, distribution, and reproduction of value in writing." The following figure provides a visualization of my subsequent corrections to that task, the output resembling not so much a rhetoric as an economy.

{% include cycle-figure.html caption="`Figure 1.` *Cohle's Pinwheel, or “Time is a flat circle”*: The economic cycle of a publication's production, distribution, use, and re-production. Arrows leaving the loop mark value appropriated at each stage; none depletes the labor stored by the text." %}

The thick loop renders time as the continuous passage of a text, which any measurement must cut into intervals prior to counting. The four boxed stages are the cuts. Production, distribution, use, and re-production are categories placed on that continuity for the convenience of theory, and drawing them is the [discretization](/weblog/2026/09/27/composition-noun-and-verb.html) writing inevitably performs. Labor enters the cycle at every stage, from different parties, as the finer arrows entering the boxes from inside the loop; value is appropriated at every stage, by different parties, as the arrows exiting the boxes. Exchange is not a stage but a form of appropriation, possible between any two stages where a text or a right to it changes hands for payment; it's the dominant form in a market that capitalism has taught us to expect between distribution and use.

### Rivalrousness and appropriability

Texts are not exhausted in the taking. Value leaves without depleting the [stored labor](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html) the text holds, and one reading subtracts nothing from another. Economists call such goods non-rivalrous, and [Thomas Jefferson](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-06-02-0322) gave the image most readers know: "he who lights his taper at mine, recieves light without darkening me" (Jefferson 1813). Jefferson concluded that nature made ideas incapable of exclusive appropriation, so that any exclusive right is society's grant.

The same text can have its value appropriated at every stage, and at each stage by more than one position. The figure also omits money, deliberately. Money asks for a step the loop never performs: one unit of a person's time set equal, at a ratio, to a different unit, and that step happens only at an exchange, between positions, with the money moving against the text. Nothing on the loop requires an exchange, so nothing on it carries a price.

### The journal, walked

Tracing the cycle of academic publication walks the loop. At production a scholar ideates and types, peers review the draft, and she or her editors edit her revisions. The publisher pays for none of this work, and most of the labor is performed by people the publisher doesn't employ. Value leaves the stage anyway: the writer takes a share, which the figure marks first; reviewers learn from what they review; an editor she hires herself draws a fee. The writer's share arrives as a credential, and the credential carries more than her labor. [Bruce Horner](https://www.jstor.org/stable/20866772) describes a *curriculum vitae* or vita in which a scholar, Mary C., claims "as her own accomplishments work that in fact she alone could not accomplish or even conceive of attempting" (Horner 2007, 167). The maker's self-appropriation suggests the position of author appropriates the surplus of everyone whose labor the article consumed on its way to her name.

At the point of distribution the publisher's staff, platform workers, and librarians contribute their labor. [John Trimbur](https://publicationsncte.org/content/journals/ccc/52/2) characterized this stage as the way "activities and abilities of publishers, editors, writers ... are themselves exchanged and, in effect, consumed in the process of production" (Trimbur 2000, 209--10). The publisher pays its own staff, copyeditors among them, and not the reviewers or the journal's academic editors. Value departs from distribution to publishers, to platforms, and to institutions, which count the publication toward a reputation.

Money most often moves between distribution and use, and exchange, by its familiarity and scale, draws attention: publishers appropriate by exchange. In 2016 RELX, Elsevier's parent company, reported adjusted operating profit of $2.875 billion on revenue of $9.377 billion, figures Jessica Reyman and I [cited](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350198.2018.1424480) (Edwards and Reyman 2018, 215), and the university that pays the author's salary pays again for the subscription. Academic publishers don't sell the text, which costs nothing to copy, but exclusion from the text, as Elsevier's Tom Reller admits, *contra* Jefferson: "[W]e can't allow published journal articles to be freely accessible on a large scale" (Reller, quoted in Edwards and Reyman 2018, 215). In our field Elsevier's journals hold an article behind the subscription for thirty-six to forty-eight months, and an author who wants the gate lifted from the start pays a fee of up to five thousand dollars (Edwards and Reyman 2018, 219) as excludability, sold back to the maker. Excludability belongs to the position and not to the text, and the market's gate is a price. [Gibson-Graham's iceberg](https://www.communityeconomies.org/resources/diverse-economies-iceberg), as I described in [*Transformation*](/weblog/2026/09/29/transformation-storage-and-time.html), illustrates the mistake that follows: the transactions above the waterline, the ones with prices, come to metonymically stand for the whole, until "the economy" means the part with receipts; the figure, therefore, draws no stage for exchange.

At the economic stage of use, students, scholars, and readers contribute their labor, and the same names stand on the appropriation side, because taking up a text is both what a reader adds and what a reader carries away. Nothing here depends on a report of what happened inside the reader.

At re-production the text enters back into production, as carried in the practices and artifacts of commentary, citation and quotation, syllabi and course packets. [Lisa Dush](https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc201527641), defining writing as content, glosses Johndan Johnson-Eilola's account of texts broken into saleable pieces with the chapter extracted from a book and republished in a coursepack whose permissions are paid, with each recombination "generating surplus value to be captured as profit" (Johnson-Eilola, quoted in Dush 2015, 178). Value leaves to the cited authors, to the proxies of rankings and h-indices that count the citations, and to the writers who argue and extend and revise. Green self-archiving returns a copy of the article to the writer and permits her to appropriate at re-production what she gave away at production. An editor in the survey Reyman and I took suggested that when her colleagues see commercial use they see stealing (Edwards and Reyman 2018, 221): an editorial board guarding its share against a commercial appropriator. Readings are never rivals; appropriators often are. The written commons under machine training shows the same phenomenon: this site's [`robots.txt`](/robots.txt) allows crawlers to copy the archive but declines training on the copies, as a form of excludability held by the maker over one use of a non-rivalrous good. The request binds only those who honor it.

That walk is the warrant for this post's law: *a party with a stake is a position in a relation, which persons occupy and leave; the surplus made at any stage of the cycle is appropriated at that stage by whoever holds the position to do so, the maker included, and by more than one party, since a text is not used up in the taking; and the share a maker takes in the making, where it is realized, no ledger can reach.*

### The wage exchange

The figure leaves out the wage exchange: the scholar sells her labor-power to the university, and the publisher takes the article produced by her labor for nothing. Marx [defines labor-power](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch06.htm) in the sixth chapter of *Capital* as the capacities a living person sets in motion "whenever he produces a use-value" (Marx 1993, 270), and the wage is the price of that capacity for a duration, paid after the fact. Marx describes how "the worker advances the use-value of his labour-power to the capitalist. He lets the buyer consume it before he receives payment of the price. Everywhere the worker allows credit to the capitalist" (Marx 1993, 278). The university pays a salary at the end of the month for capacities it consumed during it, and the scholar extends the credit.

The scholar extends more than Marx's worker. The wage buys her capacity, but the university does not purchase the article. It purchases a semester of teaching, service, and research, and it books the article as a line for promotion on the scholar's vita and a count toward its reputation. The publisher, which holds the position at which the text is appropriated by exchange, acquires the text itself through a copyright transfer signed because the journal requires one, and pays nothing for it. The money comes out elsewhere at every stage: nobody in the cycle pays the writer for the text, and the party that sells access to it never purchased it.

The surplus is made and never taken in the wage. Marx's capitalist appropriates surplus at production, by paying for labor-power and keeping the product; in the journal cycle the university pays for the labor-power and releases the product to a publisher who paid no wage. The positions are separable. The law I propose above counts a position.

### Positions without exchange

The journal cycle carries prices at exchange and nearly nowhere else. The law's third clause concerns the positions where nothing is sold. [Harriet Fraad, Stephen Resnick, and Richard Wolff](https://search.worldcat.org/isbn/9780745307084) carry the class analysis into the household, where surplus labor is performed daily and never priced: the products of a housewife's surplus labor are not sold, so her surplus labor has no exchange value (Fraad, Resnick, and Wolff 1994, 6), yet appropriation proceeds in the forms Marx named. In the feudal form the person positioned by coerction appropriates the surplus; in the communist form the group appropriates; in the ancient form "the performer and appropriator of surplus labor is the same individual" (36), who then decides where to distribute what she has herself appropriated. The ancient form provides the match: a writer who performs the surplus labor and takes the surplus in the same act.

A ledger excludes such positions. [Duncan Ironmonger](https://doi.org/10.1080/13545709610001707756), measuring Australian households in 1992, put unpaid work at 380 million hours a week against 272 million in paid employment (Ironmonger 1996, 43), and his argument examines the line the national accounts draw rather than the size of what sits past it. Ironmonger's line comes from Margaret Reid's third-person criterion of 1934: an activity counts as work if someone else could be paid to do it for you, so cooking and laundry are work, while nobody can be hired to eat a meal on your behalf, because the benefit of the activity accrues to whoever performs it. The national accounts' convention, Ironmonger writes, "ignores the *nontransferable process benefits* that accrue to the person performing the task" (Ironmonger 1996, 40). Those process benefits are the experience good under an accountant's guise: value made in the doing, accruing to the doer. The ledger stops at Reid's line because past the line nothing is exchanged, so no unit of the doer's time gets set equal to anything, and there is no ratio to inscribe. The unmeasured side is unpaid, and for the same reason unpoliced.

The share a maker takes in the making is taken at such a position, and the modal in the law's clause is deliberate. Self-appropriation is a possibility of all writing at any site, for any writer; its realization is never guaranteed, and may be rare; and the unrealized possibility is as much the object here as the realized one. The mirror holds on the market side: capitalist appropriation is never complete. The text's non-rivalrous quality keeps the shares from competing. The publisher's taking at exchange leaves the writer's share untouched because nothing was consumed in the taking, and the share is a different appropriation of the same text at a different position, not a remainder. In the *Phaedrus*, Socrates says the same about words planted by dialectic in a fitting soul, which help themselves and the one who planted them and bear seed in turn (276e--277a): the sower's return, from a seed the sower gave away.

A realized share leaves a trace at re-production. In 2016 I described an assignment in which students started the semester writing for audiences of their own time-shifted selves, their writing circulating "to be appropriated again at some future point" ([Edwards 2016](https://doi.org/10.1215/15314200-3158701), 148). The envelope opened fourteen weeks later is the classroom's version of a writer returning to an old entry, and the return is on the record either way: a later self taking up an earlier self's work, as any reader could, taking a share no one else was positioned to take.

The share cannot be an ability the writer carries. Trimbur tracked the abilities of writers, editors, and publishers into the commodity, where they circulate as traces. Horner declines composition's subsequent step: he reads the field's promise to produce skills in students as commodification, the value of a product taken for an objective property of it (Horner 2007, 164), which hides the labor of realizing value behind a thing the student carries away (172--73). [The embodied agnostic subject](/weblog/series/toward-a-theory-of-the-subject.html) stands on the line between. Abilities circulate; the share is a position the maker occupied, at a use, on a date, which the record can show. The share a maker takes in the making, where it is realized, no ledger can reach, because the ledger's reach is equivalence; the share is cut and counted and never set equal.

### Dibs, no backsies

Gibson-Graham set out to politicize positions like the one above in *[A Postcapitalist Politics](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816648047/a-postcapitalist-politics/)*. Their program for "taking back the economy" runs through resubjectivation: "the mobilization and transformation of desires, the cultivation of capacities, and the making of new identifications" (Gibson-Graham 2006, xxxvi), and they anticipate the program encountering the grief, resentment, anger, and self-hatred that attach to economic identities (143). That politics works on the interior, and [the embodied agnostic subject declines to read the interior](/weblog/2026/10/03/valuation-labor-and-measure.html). The share a maker takes can't be cultivated into being by a program, since no party can reliably read whether it was taken, the maker included; the record can show the position and the use a politics must operate upon.

Moishe Postone oriented the objection differently, and [Justin Joque](https://www.versobooks.com/products/816-revolutionary-mathematics) applies Postone's objection to the economy of automated abstraction: taking back one's alienated labor from whoever took it "can only result in a superficially improved relationship to work" (Joque 2022, chap. 8), because the form of labor under capital does the damage and a change of appropriator leaves the form standing. Joque seeks a politics that does not touch the subject and detaches from the desire to produce or reproduce subjects. The law aligns with him to that extent, but parts where the share is concerned.

The share the third clause names is not reclaimed. The writer's share was never in the publisher's possession to recover, so Postone's objection cannot apply: the share is a position without exchange, not an improved relationship to a job. Nor does the share require resubjectivation, as the exchange line protects it. Capital's indifference to the interior is a silence of uninterest: what the worker feels changes nothing about what is extracted. The agnostic clause's silence is a refusal of jurisdiction: what the writer feels is nobody's to report, her assessor's least of all. The first silence serves extraction. The second keeps the share beyond every appropriator's reach.

### Output capacity and commensurability

[Hannah Forsyth](https://crookedtimber.org/2026/08/04/if-you-cant-be-bothered-writing-it-i-cant-be-bothered-reading-it/) wrote this summer that her reading time is scarcer than she wishes, and the pressure to produce has turned academics into producers of content whose output was counted, then ranked by the outlets it appeared in, then scored for impact, each metric internalized by the people it measured and then by their students. The machine-written essay and the machine-written newsletter arrive at the end of that chain. They spend the reader's time, and in Forsyth's words, "If you can't be bothered writing it, I can't be bothered reading it." A position declines a use, and the law has nothing to add, since a reader with a stake is the party who can decline. Forsyth files the arrangement under what Pete Chalmers calls the thru-put model of higher education, a system built to pump students through at scale, scalable because its labor costs are trivial, and she renders the system through Chalmers as a wealth transfer racket: whether learning happens or not, the students get indebted and the profits get paid.

The thru-put model is the journal cycle's ledger applied to a degree. It books the path, in credit hours and tuition and headcount, and never the learning, which sits past (Margaret) Reid's line where no third person can be paid to do it. The money moves, the count is presented as a valuation, and the share a student takes in the making goes unreached, if taken. The ledger holds Thamus's position: it judges an art it cannot practice, sets the price of what it receives, and discounts the gift by the same right (274e--275a). It isn't wrong about what it books. It's writing, though, and so the target of Socrates' charge: like the figures in a painting it looks alive, and when asked what the student took, it gives the same answer every time (275d). A ledger reaches only what it can commensurate.

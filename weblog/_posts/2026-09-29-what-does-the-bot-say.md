What's frustrated me about my discipline's response to the AI panic has been the predominant refusal to define its terms and to subject it to proper analysis; to slice it up into parts and see how it works. I feel like some humanists have read Bender et al.'s stochastic parrots article with its grab-bag of complaints and taken its form as the proper model of AI critique. There's scant differentiation or specification: compositionists mostly take "AI" as a rhetorical hyperobject and try to come at it via the usual routes offered by culturalized hermeneutics. Many seem to imagine the use case as typing into a chat prompt and having the bot generate prose on a topic of choice: the essay-writing model. With some exceptions---I find John Gallagher's work on LLMs admirable---the field consigns the bot to invention without defining invention's terms and then complains the bot scores poorly on what we see as our home turf. Well, yeah. We don't need to keep congratulating ourselves for the insight that with a machine, there's no there there.

<!--more-->

I disagree with some of the critiques of the arXiv-published experiments investigating humanistic questions as uninformed, because they were written by engineers. Critiquing what an engineer built because it doesn't carry humanist values feels like critiquing a poem because it doesn't work as a calculator. Typing prose into a chatbot window does not exhaust the possibilities of technological exchange. I certainly understand the merits of constrastive rhetorics in highlighting competing value systems, but a lot of what seems to serve as contrastive rhetoric regarding language models comes across to me as humanist flag-planting.

Composition's stance largely ignores the wealth of non-invention-related, non-chat applications of what ML technologies might do for the other rhetorical canons of delivery, memory, organization, and style. The field seeks to anthropomorphize the technology into the shape of its humanist values and often misses the point: recent composition-oriented scholarship in *The Elsevier Journal that Shall Not Be Named* surveys the headlines of arXiv articles for what they say models can and can't do and hilariously *ignores the phenomena the articles were investigating*, thereby implying the position apparently under critique---that rhetorical analysis requires no evidence of human intent. Aside from salvos directed at machine style and what probabilistic models do with the shape of prose, I haven't seen many disciplinary critiques that go beyond contrastive rhetorics, the hermeneutic stance of imagined rhetorical unreliability, or that don't rely on assumptions or variables external to the questions. We can't imagine machine experiments with meaning, so we seem to have largely dismissed the possibility of constructing experiments.

I'd say when it comes to talking about language models let's please *quit complaining about meaning already* and construct some experiments. If **meaning belongs to humans**---and I take that as my first principle, so self-evident as to not require arguing---then one reasonable approach might be to isolate the variable.

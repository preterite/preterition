What's frustrated me about my discipline's response to the AI panic has been the steadfast refusal to define its terms and to subject it to proper analysis; to slice it up into parts and see how it works. Humanists seem to have read Bender et al.'s stochastic parrots article with its grab-bag of complaints and taken its form as the proper model of AI critique. There's scant differentiation or specification: compositionists mostly take "AI" as a rhetorical hyperobject and try to come at it via the usual routes of hermeneutic interpretation. Many seem to imagine the sole use case as sitting in front of a chat prompt and having the model generate prose on a topic of choice: the essay-writing model. With rare exceptions---I find John Gallagher's work on LLMs admirable---the field consigns the bot to invention without defining invention's terms and then complains that it scores poorly on what they see as their home turf.

The arXiv-published experiments aimed at humanistic questions are largely uninformed, because they were written by engineers. Critiquing what an engineer built because it doesn't carry humanist values is like critiquing a poem because it doesn't work as a calculator. Typing prose into a chatbot window does not exhaust the possibilities of technological exchange.

Composition's stance largely ignores the enormous volume of non-invention-related, non-chat applications of what ML technologies might do for the other rhetorical canons of delivery, memory, organization, and style. The field tries to anthropomorphize the technology into the shape of its humanist values and often misses the point: recent composition-oriented scholarship in *The Elsevier Journal that Shall Not Be Named* surveys the headlines of arXiv articles for what they say models can't do and hilariously *ignores the phenomena the articles were investigating*, thereby implying the position apparently under critique---that rhetorical analysis requires no evidence of human intent. Aside from salvos directed at machine style and what probabilistic models do with the shape of prose, I have yet to see disciplinary critiques that go much beyond the hermeneutic stance of imagined rhetorical unreliability or that don't rely on received opinion or variables external to the question. We can't imagine machine experiments with meaning, so we dismiss the possibility of constructing experiments.

I say let's *quit complaining about meaning already* and construct some experiments. If **meaning belongs to humans**---and I firmly believe it does---then one reasonable approach might be to isolate that variable.

Where and when do we place the writer?

In the discourse around AI and large language models (LLMs), where is the human writer, and when, and what are they doing? In other words: have we questioned the validity of the construct posited for the figure of the LLM-using human writer?

In all the scholarship I've recently read (no links in this post, because I won't give Elsevier the benefit), the human writer is placed in one location: **in front of an LLM chat window**.

Never a *code* prompt. Never an *agent* prompt. Never a *multi-agent framework*. Never an *agent orchestration* or *management platform*.

The placement in front of an LLM chat prompt ensures the construct posited matches our tidy representation of the writing situation: one writer, at one place, at one time. Doing stuff with text.

<!--more-->

If the writer is not yet writing and is reading instead, the writer is no longer a writer, but a reader, and the act takes place at a different time from the writing. Doing stuff with text. When the writer is writing, they become a writer by producing writing. The writing is produced at that moment. It becomes artifacted residue of the process, commodified or used at a moment after the writing took place.

The LLM-using human writer is imagined as an artisanal laborer engaged in piecework. If the LLM-using human writer is a student, they are imagined as apprentices to artisanal labor, producing piecework copies to be graded and discarded after making.

Writing studies imagines the situation of writing instruction as sole-authored essayistic literacy, produced and discarded. Cheating with an LLM, then, becomes a one-shot endeavor: sit down in front of a chat prompt (never a code prompt, agent prompt, multi-agent framework, *etc*.) at one time, and have the machine produce the disposable artifact. Composition's panic over LLMs forgets both the post-process movement (writing's contexts beyond its immediate situation overdetermine its production, circulation, and value) and the process movement (writing takes place over time). The LLM point of delivery---the figure of the individual reading from and typing to a box on a screen at a moment to produce a textual artifact---is an unquestioned rhetorical construct. Composition has chosen an impoverished governing metaphor for LLM writing---**the user at a chat window**---and then critiqued the impoverished situation it presents for writing.

The scholars making that critique miss themselves at the point of delivery. Their  articles travel through drafts, co-authors, reviewers, editors, and archives. One discloses delegating its formatting and sourcing to a model as if that work weren't writing. They take their attenuation of delivery for a property of the technology.

In place of the figure of the user at a chat window, and the failure of imagination it represents, I propose an alternative metaphor for the LLM writer: **the repository**.

The most common example---there are many---is GitHub. One writer makes a repo for a project or a purpose. They fill it with text. They share it, take contributions and comments. They revise and track the revisions. They document it. They take in sources from other places. They steal or cite ideas. They adapt code and text they've taken from other people and haven't verified. The value of the labor that went into it gets multiply appropriated by multiple people. Other people fork or star or link what they made. Labor adds to it, and that labor's value gets [appropriated](/weblog/2026/10/04/appropriation-position-and-relation.html) as well---by other people, in exchange and outside it, and by the writer, since that value can be *self-appropriated by the laborer over time*.

Many writers, at many places, at many times. Doing stuff with text.

Every commit, pull, and fork delivers it again. It grows. It changes. That's what writing does.

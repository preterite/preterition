Over the past decade or so, I've designed and taught and refined a 400-level English elective on ["Post-Truth Rhetorics."](https://preterite.net/resources/460-weekly-lecture-notes.pdf) It came out of an idea for a graduate seminar in the history of rhetoric themed around concepts of truth, since Rhetoric has been so frequently contrasted to Truth. I feel like the undergrad refinement is more fun---it takes one of my habitual modes of investigating a phenomenon from the definitions of its opposites.[^1] I've been occasionally delighted to overhear students, when they think I'm not around, continuing to refer to it as "the bullshit class." 

As the course and its examinations of lying and propaganda and other contestations of truth evolved, I started to see distinct strands of rhetoric to characterize as contrary. In recent history, phenomena like AIDS denialism of the Thabo Mbeki variety and Holocaust denialism in the hardcore revisionist mode seem to come under particular opprobrium in the ways they combine the rejection of three factors: 

- **consensus empiricism**, or *the reality of measurement*: the idea that repeatable, peer-reviewed measurement establishes an objective baseline that overrides personal intuition or ideological preference.

- **causal chains**, or *the reality of consequence*: the idea that causes have traceable effects and effects have traceable causes.

- **documentary recording**, or *the reality of collective memory*: the idea that shared and durable archival, forensic, and legal records may be queried for true accounts.

Students seemed excited to articulate against the course's readings the notion that rhetorical denialism, free to question any of the above factors, has recently spiked around the documentary record, as a result of the cross-media proliferation of methods of digital forgery. Such proliferation may be partly an effect of innovations in measurement technologies: it’s taken engineers only about 200 years to turn Carl Friedrich Gauss’s mathematics of measurement error into the random noise from which image generators produce counterfeit photographs. Technological change has reduced the rhetorical burden of collective memory denialism.

If one were to be a *properly* contrarian denialist, it seems like the ideal objective would be to take on the more challenging case for denialism. Which, then, might we today see as both harder and rhetorically less rewarding to pull off: causal chain denialism, or consensus empiricism denialism?

<!--more-->

In terms of effects, I'd suggest denying consensus empiricism is boring. Denying consensus measurement means arguing about calibration, sampling bias, instrument drift, denominator selection, and the like: contesting raw measurement sounds like a pedantic audit. Measurement denialism only gets fun when one abandons empiricism altogether and claims *a conspiracy so immense* is fabricating every instrument reading anywhere: at scale it shifts to collective memory denialism; limited, it's a yawner.

The effects of denying consequence are far less dull. Causation sequences narrative; causal claims tell stories with protagonists, villains, motives. The causal chain denialist gets to put on the deerstalker cap of the detective performing the hidden mechanism's hermeneutic unveil. The unveil readily flatters the intuition of the audience: "Precisely as suspected, Watson: the conventional wisdom is *[wrong](https://xkcd.com/386)*!"

Consensus empiricism requires more labor on the part of the denialist.
Empirical measurements are typically triangulated across independent instruments. One cannot effectively contest a triangulated measurement without either claiming everyone's ruler is broken or offering better measurement.

Consequence denialists take their wiggle room from the problems with inference. David Hume pointed out that we never directly *observe* causation: instead, we see one phenomenon follow another, often enough and closely enough, and infer a causal link. Complex open systems are too large and too porous to probe directly at every point, and we have no choice but to accept inference as sufficient evidence of cause. The causal chain contrarian borrows from that circumstance permanent rhetorical license, since it's possible to accept every  point of data on the table and simply propose a competing mechanism, reverse causality, or a confounding variable.

Digital fakes act as accelerants on documentary denial: when a would-be contrarian hits the wall of consensus empiricism, impugning the provenance of the archive licenses skipping past the painstaking drudgery of measurement disproof toward the floating signifiers of conspiracy theories.

|**Dimension**|**Measurement Denial (Consensus Empiricism)**|**Consequence Denial (Causal Chains)**|**Documentary Denial (Collective Memory)**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Rhetorical Posture**|_"Your ruler is broken."_|_"You're misreading the story."_|_"The receipts are fake."_|
|**Burden of Labor**|**High / Technical:** Must critique methodology, calibration, sampling, and statistical noise.|**Low / Narrative:** Can accept the data as given and re-narrate the mechanism or introduce a confounder.|**Variable / Forensic-Conspiratorial:** Requires archival labor for anomalies or sweeping claims of coordinated fabrication.|
|**Vulnerability to Reality**|**High:** Falsifiable by a single independent check, replication, or material artifact.|**Low:** Difficult to falsify cleanly in complex, multi-variable systems where causation is inferred.|**Bifurcated:** Easily refuted when provenance is verifiable; near unfalsifiable once the claim expands into totalizing conspiracy and takes counter-evidence as cover-up.|
|**Audience Appeal**|**Low ("boring"):** Sounds like bookkeeping, pedantry, or stubbornness.|**High ("sexy"):** Sounds like critical thinking, nuance, or insider pattern-recognition.|**Extreme ("taboo"):** Dramatically casts the speaker as whistleblower or parrhesiast; provokes backlash.|

While arguing over instrument calibration is tedious and easily falsified, declaring the entire archive compromised converts a dry technical dispute into a high-stakes thriller. Documentary denial is strictly falsifiable when scoped, and epistemically sealed from refutation at scales that convert the archive’s custodians into members of the imagined conspiracy. Consequence denial carries the lowest social risk because claims of alternative causation resemble conventional debate: it's cheap to stage, and only costly when the staging is revealed. Measurement denial carries reputational risk in technical fields and boredom beyond their bounds. Documentary denial carries the highest social and institutional risk, and is rarely treated as an unmotivated mistake: alleging forged records aims an indictment of fraud against the record-keepers.

Such circumstance arises partly from institutional boundaries. Perhaps interestingly, the punishments and rewards for being contrary change at the academy's gates, and vary by the field within. Some of the following examples draw on my secondhand understanding of other wings of the academy; others I feel like I grasp more firmly from experience. In the metrology-heavy physical and biomedical sciences, credibility rests on calibration, replication, and reference standards, and dismissing a consensus measurement without better instruments or an error analysis gets treated as technical incompetence. In science and technology studies (STS), critical theory, qualitative social science, and my own constructivist wing of the humanities, challenging a consensus measurement earns citations. Showing that an empirical measure disguises a situated, power-laden instrument gets rewarded as _epistemic unmasking_, and the reward carries no obligation to offer a better metric. Defending a measurement in such company earns suspicion and scant citations.

Contesting causes gets policed field by field. In disciplines with strict mechanistic pathways---molecular biology, pharmacology---denying a demonstrated mechanism without offering a better one reads as naive. In the experimental social sciences, peer reviewers will ding both the flat denial of a demonstrated link and the causal claim that lacks a clean identification strategy. Academic publishing still pays for the surprise: journals prefer original and positive results to replications, and “We showed the relationship may be inverted” is sexier than “We measured again and verified the result.”

Beyond the academy's gates, audiences for op-eds, cable news, and congressional testimony reward certainty, moral clarity, and narrative structure, and cast academics as umpires or detectives. The umpire gets consulted when the public is anxious about spun numbers or projected crisis: the epidemiologist, the forensic scientist, the audit statistician. Academics who calmly and legibly explain consensus measurement gather institutional authority. Yet the umpire's role has a limit: telling audiences that an unpopular policy delivered beneficial outcomes sounds like dismissing experience. The detective gets lionized as a myth-busting truth-teller for subverting the conventional wisdom, however far the counter-causal claim outruns the field’s evidence.

Questioning a measure the public understands as plain fact comes across as denying reality. The academy’s favored form of internal contrarianism---the act of unmasking how measurements are constructed---translates poorly into a public sphere already primed by digital fakery to believe the entire archive is rigged. By Bruno Latour’s metaphor, critique has run out of steam: the unmasking punctured the boiler. While an academic playing the umpire or the detective may feel reduced, failing to distinguish between interrogating a ruler and burning the archive leaves the field open to drama and conspiracy.

How contrary, then, does the properly contrarian denialist need to be? Contrary enough to take the more difficult case, and the more difficult case entails homework: calibration, sampling bias, denominator selection. Do the homework well and the denial becomes an audit. With collective memory cheap to contest and causal overturning the default currency, perhaps the most gloriously boring move left is to defend the measurement.

Mary may be quite contrary, but her garden offers a census: silver bells and cockleshells, and pretty maids all in a row.

### Re-Reading the Limits of Linguistic Abstraction

Conference on College Composition and Communication, Baltimore MD, 12 April
2025
{: .card-meta}

First-year composition pedagogies attempting to respond to the crisis of
textual proliferation prompted by Large Language Models (LLMs) fail to
adequately address the circumstance "that words have a life of their own,
radio-controlled by an automatic pilot called history," in Charles
Bernstein's 1987 words: "To understand language as artificial intelligence is
to conceptualize writing as a kind of psychic surgery." Compositionists might
productively imagine writing that, via Bernstein, "sounds as music the marks
of its continual newness in being made; and the only mark of its past, of its
having been made, is the blood on the cutting room floor." Emerging
technologies simultaneously obscure and reveal tensions between abstracted
knowledge processes and material-historical human labor.

### Questioning Generative AI: How to Not Write Like a Robot

Featured presentation, Conference on College Composition and Communication,
Spokane WA, 04 April 2024
{: .card-meta}

Composition's reception of generative AI has been shaped by a disciplinary wariness of numbers. That wariness costs the field an insight it already owned: Peter Elbow's published characterization of the reading process describes the predictive machinery of a language model in composition's vocabulary, with parallels easily traceable to the mathematical processes of stochastic gradient descent in Large Language Models (LLMs). I use Mariolina Salvatori's diagnosis of the double erasure of the reading student and of that student's reading processes to argue that LLMs are better understood as 'two-player word processors' than as producers of pre-completed and pre-plagiarized texts, and that their failures are precisely the sites of the appropriation of the value of students' writing labor.

### Owning Your Digital Shadow: Students' Right to Their Own Data

Computers and Writing, George Mason University, 25 May 2018
{: .card-meta}

In 2017, CCCC Chair Linda Adler-Kassner gestured toward the problem of
educational paradata, the recorded "data exhaust" of student work that under
US law belongs to the collector rather than the producer. Framing educational
paradata as a technological by-product rather than an authored text makes it
appropriable at the moment of creation. Adler-Kassner's framing presumes the
appropriation of value by parties other than the creator and blames the
appropriation of value on the quantitative measurement of value, manifesting
what Victor Villanueva has referred to as a humanist fear of numbers, and
ignoring the productive uses of measurement as indices of the appropriation
of value by the producer or by other parties. I offer a case for data
sovereignty: the right to download, own, analyze, and argue from one's own
record of composing.
